# Handout 1: The Turtle Bayou Resolutions

**What this is:** A list of grievances and resolutions written by armed Texian settlers in June 1832, right after they had taken up arms against a Mexican garrison commander — but framed not as rebellion against Mexico, instead as loyal support for a Mexican general fighting to restore the Constitution of 1824.

---

## Provenance Box

| | |
|---|---|
| **Author** | A committee of Texian settlers (named signers below), acting on behalf of "a large and respectable meeting" of citizens of the Jurisdiction of Austin & Liberty |
| **Date** | June 13, 1832 |
| **Place written** | A camp at Turtle Bayou, near Anahuac, in present-day Chambers County, Texas |
| **Audience** | Formally addressed to **Col. José Antonio Mexía**, a Mexican officer supporting Santa Anna's federalist movement against the Bustamante administration — but written to be published and read by the wider Texas settler public |
| **How we have it** | This document survives as part of the **Mirabeau B. Lamar Papers** at the Texas State Library and Archives Commission (TSLAC). It was also published in a Brazoria newspaper in July 1832. What TSLAC holds and has transcribed is titled "Address to Colonel José Antonio Mexía, June 13, 1832 (Turtle Bayou Resolutions)" — it functions as both a letter to Mexía explaining the settlers' actions and the resolutions themselves. |

## The Text (verbatim, TSLAC transcript)

> Col. Jose Antonio Mexia
>
> Sir
>
> Having understood that the causes which impelled us to take up arms have been misrepresented, or misunderstood, we therefore make you the following representation.
>
> The Colonists of Texas have long since been convinced of the arbitrary, and unconstitutional measures, of the administration of Bustamente, as evidenced
>
> **First** By their repeated violations of the constitution, laws, and their total disregard of the civil & political rights of the people.
>
> **2** By their fixing and establishing among us, in time of peace, military posts, the object of which totally disregarding the local civil authorities of the state, and by committing various acts, which evinced, [oppositidity?] to the true interests of the people in the enjoyment of civil liberty.
>
> **3** By the arrest of Juan Francisco Madero, the commissioner on the part of the state Government to put the inhabitants east of the River Trinity, in possession of their lands in conformity with the laws of colonization.
>
> **4** By the interposition of a military force, preventing the Alcalde of the Jurisdiction of Liberty from the exercise of his constitutional functions.
>
> **5** By appointing to the revenue department of Galveston, a man whose character for infamy had been clearly established, and made known to the Government, and whose principles were avowedly inimical to the true interest of the people of Texas.
>
> **6** By the military commandant of Anahuac, advising & procuring servants to exit the Service of their masters — offering them protection, causing them to labour for his individual benefit by force and refusing to compensate master or Servant.
>
> **7** By the imprisonment of our citizens without lawful cause, and claiming the right of trying said citizens, by a military court, for offenses of a character alone cognizable by the civil authority, and by refusing to deliver them over to the said authority when demanded.
>
> Such Colonel Mexia are the causes which impelled us to take up our arms, and the following declarations are the legitimate offspring of our deliberations and form the basis of all the acts.
>
> At a large and respectable meeting of the citizens residents of the Jurisdiction of Austin & Liberty held at Turtle Bayou near Anahuac the following resolutions were reported by the committee appointed by the meeting and unanimously adopted:
>
> **Resolved** That we view with feelings of the deepest regret the manner in which the Govt of the Republic of Mexico is administered by the present dinasty [sic] — The repeated violations of the constitution — the total disregard of the laws — the entire prostration of the civil authority; and the substitution in its stead of a military despotism, are greivances [sic] of such character, as to arouse the feelings of every freeman, and impel him to resistance.
>
> **Resolved** That we view with feelings of the deepest interest, and solicitude, the firm manly resistance, which is made by the highly talented and distinguished Cheiftan [sic] — General Santa Anna, to the numberless encroachments and infractions, which have been made by the present administration, upon the Constitution and laws of our adopted and beloved country.
>
> **Resolved** That as freemen devoted to a correct interpretation, and enforcement of the constitution, and laws, according to their true Spirit — We pledge our lives and fortunes in support of the same, and of the distinguished leader who is now so gallantly fighting in defence of civil liberty.
>
> **Resolved** That the people of Texas be invited to cooperate with us in support of the principles incorporated in the foregoing resolutions.
>
> 13th June 1832
>
> Signed, the following committee on behalf of the meeting:
>
> Wyly Martin
> John Austin
> Luke Lessasier
> William H. Jack
> Hugh B. Johnston
> Francis W. Johnson
> Robert M. Williamson

*Note: "[sic]" marks original spellings ("dinasty," "greivances," "Cheiftan") preserved exactly as TSLAC transcribed them from the manuscript — these are the writers' own spelling, not typing errors introduced here. The bracketed "[oppositidity?]" is TSLAC's own transcription mark, showing where the manuscript is hard to read even for the archivists who transcribed it; nothing has been invented to fill that gap.*

## Glossary

- **Impelled** — pushed or driven to do something
- **Arbitrary** — based on one person's will or whim rather than law or fairness
- **Unconstitutional** — against the constitution (in this case, Mexico's Constitution of 1824)
- **Prostration** — being completely knocked down or crushed (here, meaning civil authority had been totally overridden)
- **Despotism** — rule by a single person with total, unchecked power
- **Alcalde** — a local Mexican town magistrate/mayor, combining judicial and administrative duties
- **Commandant** — the military officer in charge of a post or garrison
- **Cognizable** — able to be judged or tried (a legal term — "cognizable by civil authority" means only a civilian court, not a military one, should handle it)
- **Colonization laws** — Mexican laws governing how land grants and settlement worked in Texas
- **Federalist** — someone who supports strong state/regional government power, as opposed to a strong central government (centralist)

## Three Sourcing Questions

1. This document is framed as loyal support for Santa Anna and the Constitution of 1824 — yet the same men had just taken up arms against a Mexican garrison commander (Bradburn) at Anahuac. Knowing that Santa Anna would later become the centralist dictator these same colonists rebelled against in 1835-36, how should that later history change the way you read the loyalty language in this 1832 document? Does it make the 1832 declarations dishonest, or does it show people responding sincerely to the information and leaders in front of them at the time?
2. The resolutions list seven specific grievances (numbered 1 through 7) before the "Resolved" statements. Why might the committee have chosen to lead with a long list of legal and administrative complaints — arrests, military courts, an interfered-with land commissioner — rather than jumping straight to "we support Santa Anna"? What does that ordering suggest about who they were trying to convince, and of what?
3. Grievance #6 accuses the Anahuac commandant of encouraging enslaved people to leave "the Service of their masters" for his own benefit — not on any principled anti-slavery grounds, but because he supposedly exploited them for his own labor. This document was written by settlers, several of whom held people in slavery. How does this specific accusation complicate the idea that this was purely a dispute about "liberty" and "constitutional rights" in the abstract? What does it reveal about whose liberty the authors meant?

## Teacher Note

**What's contested:** The Anahuac disturbance of 1832 is sometimes retold as an early, clean example of "Texans fighting for freedom." The resolutions themselves complicate that: this is a document of loyal opposition, not separatism — the drafters go out of their way to praise Santa Anna and Mexico ("our adopted and beloved country") and frame their armed resistance as *support* for the existing constitutional order, not a break from it. Students should not read Turtle Bayou as an independence document; it is the opposite in its stated intent, even though historians see it in hindsight as an early rehearsal for arguments that would resurface in 1835-36.

**What students commonly misread:** Students often assume "resolutions against a military commander" automatically means "we want independence." This document explicitly does not say that — it says the opposite, and it is worth having students find and underline the specific lines that profess loyalty to Mexico and to Santa Anna.

**The one thing worth saying out loud:** Six months after signing this, some of these same men could not have known Santa Anna would abolish the very constitution they were pledging their "lives and fortunes" to defend. This is a genuinely useful lesson in how historical actors make sincere commitments based on incomplete information — and how a document's *stated* purpose (loyalty) can look very different in hindsight once you know what came next.
