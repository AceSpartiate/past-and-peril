# Handout 7: The Goliad Orders — Santa Anna, Urrea, and Portilla

**What this is:** Three Mexican officers' own words about the single deadliest event of the Texas Revolution — the March 27, 1836 execution of surrendered Texian prisoners at Goliad. This handout is unusual: it puts the commanding general who ordered the killings, the field general who tried to stop them, and the officer who actually carried them out side by side, in their own recorded words.

> **A note on the numbers, because they differ across sources and students will notice.** Portilla's
> diary records **445 prisoners** assembled that morning. The number actually **executed** is
> usually given as **roughly 342**, and estimates range from about 340 to over 400. The gap is
> real: physicians, interpreters, and skilled workers were spared, a number of men were hidden by
> **Francita Alavez** and others, and about **28 escaped** during the shooting.
>
> **This discrepancy is worth showing students rather than smoothing over.** It is a clean, concrete
> example of TEKS 7.20(F) — why careful historians write "approximately," and why a source that
> gives one confident number without a range deserves a second look.

## The Text

**1. From General José de Urrea's diary, entry for March 27, 1836** (VERIFIED VERBATIM):

> On the 27th, between nine and ten in the morning, I received a communication from Lieut. Col. Portilla, military commandant of that point, telling me that he had received orders from His Excellency, the general-in-chief, to shoot all the prisoners and that he was making preparations to fulfill the order. This order was received by Portilla at seven in the evening of the 26th, and although he notified me of the fact on that same date, his communication did not reach me until after the execution had been carried out. All the members of my division were distressed to hear this news, and I no less, being as sensitive as my companions who will bear testimony of my excessive grief. [...]
>
> These orders always seemed to me harsh, but they were the inevitable result of the barbarous and inhuman decree which declared outlaws those whom it wished to convert into citizens of the republic. Strange inconsistency in keeping with the confusion that characterized the times! I wished to elude these orders as far as possible without compromising my personal responsibility; and, with this object in view, I issued several orders to Lieut. Col. Portilla, instructing him to use the prisoners for the rebuilding of Goliad. [...] It was painful to me, also, that so many brave men should thus be sacrificed, particularly the much esteemed and fearless Fannin. [...] I used my influence with the general-in-chief to save them, if possible, from being butchered, particularly Fannin. I obtained from His Excellency only a severe reply, repeating his previous order [...]. Even after this lamentable event, I still received a letter of the general-in-chief, dated on the 26th, saying: "I say nothing regarding the prisoners, for I have already stated what their fate shall be when taken with arms in their hands."

**2. From Lieut. Col. José Nicolás de la Portilla's own diary, entries for March 26–27, 1836** (VERIFIED VERBATIM):

> **March 26.** At seven in the evening I received orders from General Santa Anna by special messenger, instructing me to execute at once all prisoners taken by force of arms agreeable to the general orders on the subject. (I have the original order in my possession.) I kept the matter secret and no one knew of it except Col. Garay, to whom I communicated the order. At eight o'clock, on the same night, I received a communication from Gen. Urrea by special messenger in which among other things he says, "Treat the prisoners well, especially Fannin. Keep them busy rebuilding the town and erecting a fort. Feed them with the cattle you will receive from Refugio." What a cruel contrast in these opposite instructions! I spent a restless night.
>
> **March 27.** At daybreak, I decided to carry out the orders of the general-in-chief because I considered them superior. I assembled the whole garrison and ordered the prisoners, who were still sleeping, to be awaked. There were 445. [...] I gave instructions to these officers to carry out the orders of the supreme government and the general-in-chief. This was immediately done. There was a great contrast in the feelings of the officers and the men. Silence prevailed. Sad at heart I wrote to Gen. Urrea expressing my regret at having been concerned in so painful an affair.

Elsewhere, Portilla is quoted writing directly to Urrea: "I feel much distressed at what has occurred here; a scene enacted in cold blood having passed before my eyes which has filled me with horror. All I can say is, that my duty as a soldier, and what I owe to my country, must be my guaranty."

**3. Santa Anna's order to Portilla — EXCERPT VERIFIED WITH MODERATE CONFIDENCE, NOT INDEPENDENTLY VIEWED IN THE ORIGINAL ARCHIVE.** Two independent secondary sources quote Santa Anna's dispatch to the Goliad commander in identical wording, both citing Castañeda's 1928 translation, but neither source we could directly fetch reproduces a scanned/archival original for us to check word-for-word. Because this document matters enormously for precision, we are flagging it rather than presenting it as fully confirmed:

> "As the supreme government has ordered that all foreigners taken with arms in their hands, making war upon the nation, shall be treated as pirates, I have been surprised that the circular of the said government has not been fully complied with in this particular. I therefore order that you should give immediate effect to the said ordinance in respect to all those foreigners who have yielded to the force of arms..."

**A teacher who wants to print this exact passage should first verify it against a library copy of Castañeda's *The Mexican Side of the Texas Revolution* (1928) before handing it to students as verbatim** — everything else on this handout has been independently fetched and confirmed; this passage has not, and is included here only because it is widely and consistently quoted with the same wording across independent sources.

**Corroborating eyewitness account** (VERIFIED VERBATIM), from **Ramón Martínez Caro**, Santa Anna's own personal secretary, describing what happened when Urrea asked for leniency (also translated by Castañeda, via the same Sons of DeWitt Colony compilation):

> "His Excellency strongly reprimanded him [Gen. Urrea], expressing his displeasure and commanding him at the same time not to soil his triumphs with a mistaken display of generosity. He ordered him to execute all the prisoners."

## Glossary

- **General-in-chief** — the supreme commander of the whole army; here, Santa Anna
- **Decree** — an official order issued by a government with the force of law (here, the December 30, 1835 law treating armed foreign fighters as "pirates," subject to execution)
- **Outlaws / pirates** — in this context, a legal label Mexico applied to armed Anglo-American Texian fighters, denying them status as legitimate prisoners of war
- **Capitulation** — a formal agreement of surrender, often including terms/conditions
- **Commandant** — the officer in charge of a specific military post
- **Sappers** — soldiers or laborers used for engineering/construction work (Urrea wanted the prisoners kept alive to do this work instead of being executed)
- **Compromising my personal responsibility** — Urrea's way of saying he tried to avoid direct blame or disobedience while still resisting an order he found unjust
- **"Agreeable to the general orders"** — old-fashioned phrasing meaning "in accordance with" or "following"

## Three Sourcing Questions

1. Urrea wrote his diary partly to defend his own reputation after the war — he was widely blamed (fairly or not) for the massacre because it happened in his territory of command. Knowing that Urrea had a personal stake in appearing sympathetic to the prisoners, how does that change how much weight you'd give his account of trying to save them? Does the fact that Portilla's diary — written by a different man, under different pressure — independently confirms Urrea sent orders to "treat the prisoners well" make Urrea's account more believable?
2. Portilla wrote in his diary that he "decided to carry out the orders of the general-in-chief because I considered them superior" to Urrea's contradictory instructions. He had two direct orders from two different generals that flatly contradicted each other. What does his choice — and the fact that he wrote about his own distress doing it — tell you about the chain of command problems in the Mexican army, and about how a soldier can feel forced to do something he privately regrets?
3. The one Santa Anna quotation on this handout that could NOT be independently verified against an original document is also the most quotable, most widely repeated line about Goliad on the internet. Why should a historian (or a 7th grader) be *more* suspicious of a quote precisely because it's dramatic and gets repeated everywhere, rather than less? What would you want to see before you fully trusted it?

## Teacher Note

**What's contested:** The core facts are not seriously disputed by historians: Santa Anna ordered the executions, overriding Urrea's explicit attempts to save the prisoners, and Portilla carried the order out. What is genuinely a matter of ongoing historical judgment is how much personal moral credit Urrea deserves — some historians see him as a genuine moral actor constrained by military hierarchy; others note he still executed prisoners on his own initiative on other occasions during the campaign (see his own diary entry about authorizing "the execution... of thirty adventurers taken prisoners" earlier in the campaign) and caution against turning him into an uncomplicated hero.

**What students commonly misread:** Students often assume "Santa Anna ordered it" means every Mexican soldier and officer wanted it to happen. The documentary record shows real disagreement and anguish within the Mexican chain of command itself — Portilla's own diary describes a "restless night" and "silence" among his men. This does not erase what happened; it complicates the idea of a uniformly villainous "enemy."

**The one thing worth saying out loud:** A soldier receiving two direct, contradictory orders from two different generals on the same night, and having to decide which one to obey knowing men's lives depend on the choice, is a genuinely useful scenario for students to sit with — not to excuse Portilla, but to understand that atrocities are usually carried out through ordinary chains of command, by people who leave a paper trail of their own discomfort, rather than by cartoonish villains reveling in cruelty.
