# Two Letters, One Man: Stephen F. Austin Changes His Mind

**What this is:** Two real documents written by the same Texas colonizer, three and a half years apart — one begging colonists to be patient and cooperate with Mexico, the other declaring that war is the only option left. Reading them side by side shows a real person's mind changing under pressure, not a simple "hero always wanted independence" story.

> **Correcting a popular myth:** Many textbooks and websites say Austin declared "war is our only recourse" in a *speech* at a Brazoria banquet on September 8, 1835. Research for this handout traced the actual wording to a *written circular* Austin signed eleven days later, on **September 19, 1835**, and the real word is "**resource**," not "recourse." His actual September 8 banquet speech (also in the historical record) is far more cautious — it calls only for a "general consultation" of the people, not war. That speech is not reprinted here because it would not serve the "same man reversing course" comparison as clearly as the true chronology below.

## Document A — The Moderate: Austin to Samuel M. Williams

### Provenance
- **Author:** Stephen F. Austin
- **Date:** April 9, 1832
- **Place written:** Leona Vicario (the temporary name of Saltillo, Coahuila y Texas), where Austin was attending the state legislature
- **Audience:** Samuel May Williams, Austin's personal secretary and business partner in San Felipe de Austin — a private letter, not meant for the public
- **How we have it:** Original manuscript held in the Williams Papers, Rosenberg Library, Galveston, Texas. Printed as a transcribed document in *The Austin Papers*, ed. Eugene C. Barker (a scholarly documentary edition), Annual Report of the American Historical Association for 1919, Vol. II, pp. 762–764.

### The Text (excerpt, spelling and punctuation preserved from the printed transcription)

> Leona Vicario 9 April 1832
>
> Dr Sir.
>
> [...] *[Cut: opening paragraphs on routine legislative business in Saltillo — a commercial law, and political maneuvering between the governor, "Santana" (Santa Anna), and the ministers in Mexico City. Cut because it is local political detail not needed for the point of the letter.]*
>
> On my arrival here I found rumors in great abundance relative to Texas — that it had separated and declared independence etc. etc. The Gov[erno]r did not believe anything of the kind, and I have fully satisfied him[.] [...] The object is a very important one, and it is best to bear almost any thing rather than jeapordise all by rashness and ill timed passion and imprudence. All the wild sayings and gabbling over cups and speeches etc at Brazoria have been circulated over this country with wonderfull augmentations. The fact is that the great majority of the thinking and intelligent part of the people in the towns I passed through and in this place are in favor of the colonists but they fear the settlers will ruin themselves by their imprudence. If they are unjustly oppressed and can so make it appear the majority of the whole nation will be in their favor, but if the reverse of this is the case, and they fly into a fit of passion for slight causes the whole nation will be against them.
>
> The advice I have rec[eive]d. from a high source is, as follows — "Harmonize as much as possible with the military — give no cause for disgust — be calm and never shew any passion — never use threats or harsh language. [...] No violence must be used on any pretext — no imprudent talking etc."
>
> Better advice could not have been given and if properly followed will produce favourable results. Do try and impress this on everyone, and especially on those in Brazoria who are rather warmer than they ought to be, tho perhaps not much more so than rigid justice requires.
>
> Some men in the world hold the doctrine that it is degrading and corrupt to use policy in anything. Without saying whether I approve of this doctrine or not, I do say that **there is no degradation in prudence and a well tempered and well timed moderation.** The absolute freedom of speech that is used in the U.S. of the North is unknown in this country and hence it is not properly understood, and for this reason [...] complaints when made in harsh language are construed into rebellion etc. etc.
>
> [...] *[Cut: closing lines about a land-law amendment, a family greeting, and instructions to show the letter to "John Austin." Cut as routine closing business.]*
>
> S. F. Austin

## Document B — The War Circular: Austin to the Committee of Safety, Columbia

### Provenance
- **Author:** Stephen F. Austin, as Chairman of the Committee of Safety of the Jurisdiction of Austin
- **Date:** September 19, 1835 (eleven days after his Brazoria homecoming speech, and about two weeks before the first shots of the Texas Revolution at Gonzales on October 2, 1835)
- **Place written:** San Felipe de Austin
- **Document type:** A **circular** — a public bulletin meant to be copied and forwarded to committees across the Texas settlements, not a private letter and not the Brazoria dinner speech
- **Audience:** The Committee of Safety at Columbia, Texas, and — since it was meant to be copied — every settlement it reached
- **How we have it:** Surviving as a manuscript copy; printed in *The Austin Papers*, ed. Eugene C. Barker, Vol. II, pp. 128–129. Circulars like this one from the same committee in this period are independently documented as having also been issued as printed handbills and reprinted in the newspaper the *Texas Republican*.

### The Text (excerpt, spelling and punctuation preserved from the printed transcription)

> CIRCULAR from the committee of safety of the Jurisdiction of Austin
>
> Information of the most important and decisive character has just been received from Bexar from unquestionable authority which in the opinion of this committee calls for the prompt attention of the people.
>
> [...] *[Cut: a paragraph naming General Cos's troop movements and an alleged Mexican plan to "foment divisions and discord among the people." Cut for length; the point — that reconciliation looked hopeless — is restated below.]*
>
> This Committee deems it to be a duty to say that in its opinion all kind of conciliatory measures with Gen[era]l Cos and the military at Bexar are hopeless, and that nothing but ruin to Texas can be expected from any such measures — They have already and very properly been resorted to without effect.
>
> **WAR is our only resource — there is no other remedy but to defend our rights, our country and ourselves by force of arms** — To do this we must be united, and in order to unite, the delegates of the people must meet in general consultation and organize a system of defense and give organization to the country so as to produce concert —
>
> UNTILL some competent authority is established to direct all that can be done is to recommend[;] this Committee therefore earnestly recommends this subject and advises every man in Texas to prepare for war and lay aside all hopes of conciliation.
>
> San Felipe de Austin September 19 1835
>
> S. F. Austin chairman of the Comm.
>
> Please to send copies to Matagorda and over the country.

## Glossary

| Term | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| Prudence | Careful, level-headed judgment before acting |
| Imprudence / imprudent | Acting carelessly or without thinking things through |
| Conciliatory / conciliation | Trying to smooth things over and avoid a fight |
| Jeopardize | To put something at risk of being lost or ruined |
| Committee of Safety | A local emergency committee towns set up to organize defense and communication |
| Circular | A letter or notice written to be copied and passed from town to town |
| Consultation | Here, a planned meeting of elected delegates from all the Texas settlements (which became the Consultation of 1835) |
| Concert (as used here) | Coordinated, unified action — not music |
| Chairman of the Comm. | Abbreviation for "Committee" — Austin signed as the head of his local safety committee |

## Three Sourcing Questions

1. Document A was a **private** letter to a friend, and Document B was a **public** circular meant to be copied and read aloud across many towns. How might Austin have written differently knowing who would (or wouldn't) read each one? What does that tell you about being careful with "private" versus "public" historical documents?
2. In Document A, Austin criticizes "the wild sayings and gabbling over cups and speeches etc at Brazoria." In Document B, he is the one now calling for war. What specific things changed between April 1832 and September 1835 (in Mexico's government, and in what Austin personally experienced) that might explain the shift? Is it fair to call this a change of belief, or a change of strategy in response to new facts?
3. Document B was written by Austin **as chairman of a committee**, not just as an individual colonist. Does writing in an official role change how much we should trust the document as a personal, honest reflection of what Austin believed — or does it make it more reliable as a record of what he wanted people to believe?

## Teacher Note

**What's contested / commonly misread:** The phrase "war is our only recourse" is repeated constantly in textbooks, museum placards, and websites, almost always attached to Austin's September 8, 1835 Brazoria homecoming speech. Research for this handout could not verify that wording in that speech — the actual Brazoria speech text (found in *The Austin Papers*, Vol. III) is measured and calls only for a "general consultation," explicitly stating Austin advised Mexican officials against sending troops but never calling for war outright. The line students usually associate with that moment — **"WAR is our only resource"** — instead comes from a written circular Austin signed eleven days later, on September 19, 1835, and the original word is "resource," not "recourse." This is a good live example for students of how a famous quote can drift from its actual source through repetition. Say this out loud in class: **the popularized quote and its usual date/context are not what the primary sources actually show — this is exactly the kind of gap 7.20(F) asks you to notice.**

Students commonly assume Austin was always either "for the Revolution" or "against it." These two documents show a real, specific reversal driven by escalating events (Santa Anna's move toward centralized power, the imprisonment of allies, and the approach of Mexican troops toward the colonies) — not a hidden secret plan.
