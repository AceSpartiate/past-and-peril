# Handout 6: Texas Declaration of Independence — Grievances (March 2, 1836)

**What this is:** The formal document by which delegates of Texas declared their independence from Mexico, adopted on March 2, 1836 — most of it is a long, specific list of grievances against the Mexican government, written to justify the break to the outside world.

---

## Provenance Box

| | |
|---|---|
| **Author** | Drafted primarily by **George C. Childress**, chairman of the five-man drafting committee, then debated and adopted by the full Convention |
| **Date** | March 2, 1836 |
| **Place** | Washington-on-the-Brazos, Texas, where 59 delegates had gathered for the "Convention of 1836" |
| **Audience** | Explicitly "an impartial world" (other nations whose recognition Texas wanted), and also the Texian public and the Mexican government itself |
| **How we have it** | The Convention adopted and signed an official engrossed copy, which was printed immediately and distributed. The signed original is preserved by the Texas State Library and Archives Commission. The transcript below comes from the Avalon Project at Yale Law School, a widely used scholarly digital archive of historical legal documents. |

## The Text — verbatim, excerpted

*(The opening includes several paragraphs of general political philosophy about when a people has the right to revolt. Only the first and last of these are kept here — the full grievance list, which follows, is the pedagogically central part of this document and is reproduced in full.)*

> When a government has ceased to protect the lives, liberty and property of the people, from whom its legitimate powers are derived, and for the advancement of whose happiness it was instituted, and so far from being a guarantee for the enjoyment of those inestimable and inalienable rights, becomes an instrument in the hands of evil rulers for their oppression.

**[...] — omitted: two paragraphs** arguing that Mexico's federal constitution "no longer has a substantial existence" and that when "moderation is at length so far lost by those in power" that even "the forms themselves of the constitution" are discarded, a people may resist by force — dense 19th-century political-philosophy prose that restates, at greater length, the same "right of revolution" idea as the paragraph above.

> When, in consequence of such acts of malfeasance and abdication on the part of the government, anarchy prevails, and civil society is dissolved into its original elements. In such a crisis, the first law of nature, the right of self-preservation, the inherent and inalienable rights of the people to appeal to first principles, and take their political affairs into their own hands in extreme cases, enjoins it as a right towards themselves, and a sacred obligation to their posterity, to abolish such government, and create another in its stead, calculated to rescue them from impending dangers, and to secure their future welfare and happiness.
>
> Nations, as well as individuals, are amenable for their acts to the public opinion of mankind. A statement of a part of our grievances is therefore submitted to an impartial world, in justification of the hazardous but unavoidable step now taken, of severing our political connection with the Mexican people, and assuming an independent attitude among the nations of the earth.
>
> The Mexican government, by its colonization laws, invited and induced the Anglo-American population of Texas to colonize its wilderness under the pledged faith of a written constitution, that they should continue to enjoy that constitutional liberty and republican government to which they had been habituated in the land of their birth, the United States of America.
>
> In this expectation they have been cruelly disappointed, inasmuch as the Mexican nation has acquiesced in the late changes made in the government by General Antonio Lopez de Santa Anna, who having overturned the constitution of his country, now offers us the cruel alternative, either to abandon our homes, acquired by so many privations, or submit to the most intolerable of all tyranny, the combined despotism of the sword and the priesthood.

**The full list of grievances (nothing cut):**

> It has sacrificed our welfare to the state of Coahuila, by which our interests have been continually depressed through a jealous and partial course of legislation, carried on at a far distant seat of government, by a hostile majority, in an unknown tongue, and this too, notwithstanding we have petitioned in the humblest terms for the establishment of a separate state government, and have, in accordance with the provisions of the national constitution, presented to the general Congress a republican constitution, which was, without just cause, contemptuously rejected.
>
> It incarcerated in a dungeon, for a long time, one of our citizens, for no other cause but a zealous endeavor to procure the acceptance of our constitution, and the establishment of a state government.
>
> It has failed and refused to secure, on a firm basis, the right of trial by jury, that palladium of civil liberty, and only safe guarantee for the life, liberty, and property of the citizen.
>
> It has failed to establish any public system of education, although possessed of almost boundless resources, (the public domain,) and although it is an axiom in political science, that unless a people are educated and enlightened, it is idle to expect the continuance of civil liberty, or the capacity for self government.
>
> It has suffered the military commandants, stationed among us, to exercise arbitrary acts of oppression and tyrrany [sic], thus trampling upon the most sacred rights of the citizens, and rendering the military superior to the civil power.
>
> It has dissolved, by force of arms, the state Congress of Coahuila and Texas, and obliged our representatives to fly for their lives from the seat of government, thus depriving us of the fundamental political right of representation.
>
> It has demanded the surrender of a number of our citizens, and ordered military detachments to seize and carry them into the Interior for trial, in contempt of the civil authorities, and in defiance of the laws and the constitution.
>
> It has made piratical attacks upon our commerce, by commissioning foreign desperadoes, and authorizing them to seize our vessels, and convey the property of our citizens to far distant ports for confiscation.
>
> It denies us the right of worshipping the Almighty according to the dictates of our own conscience, by the support of a national religion, calculated to promote the temporal interest of its human functionaries, rather than the glory of the true and living God.
>
> It has demanded us to deliver up our arms, which are essential to our defence, the rightful property of freemen, and formidable only to tyrannical governments.
>
> It has invaded our country both by sea and by land, with intent to lay waste our territory, and drive us from our homes; and has now a large mercenary army advancing, to carry on against us a war of extermination.
>
> It has, through its emissaries, incited the merciless savage, with the tomahawk and scalping knife, to massacre the inhabitants of our defenseless frontiers.
>
> It hath been, during the whole time of our connection with it, the contemptible sport and victim of successive military revolutions, and hath continually exhibited every characteristic of a weak, corrupt, and tyrranical [sic] government.

> These, and other grievances, were patiently borne by the people of Texas, untill [sic] they reached that point at which forbearance ceases to be a virtue. We then took up arms in defence of the national constitution. We appealed to our Mexican brethren for assistance. Our appeal has been made in vain. Though months have elapsed, no sympathetic response has yet been heard from the Interior. We are, therefore, forced to the melancholy conclusion, that the Mexican people have acquiesced in the destruction of their liberty, and the substitution therfor of a military government; that they are unfit to be free, and incapable of self government.
>
> The necessity of self-preservation, therefore, now decrees our eternal political separation.
>
> We, therefore, the delegates with plenary powers of the people of Texas, in solemn convention assembled, appealing to a candid world for the necessities of our condition, do hereby resolve and declare, that our political connection with the Mexican nation has forever ended, and that the people of Texas do now constitute a free, Sovereign, and independent republic, and are fully invested with all the rights and attributes which properly belong to independent nations; and, conscious of the rectitude of our intentions, we fearlessly and confidently commit the issue to the decision of the Supreme arbiter of the destinies of nations.

## Glossary

- **Inalienable** — a right that cannot be taken away or given up, even by the person who has it
- **Malfeasance** — wrongdoing by someone in a position of official power
- **Despotism** — rule by a single person or small group with total, unchecked power
- **Palladium** — something believed to protect or guarantee a value (here, trial by jury is called the "palladium of civil liberty")
- **Commandant** — the military officer in charge of a post or garrison
- **Piratical** — like piracy; attacking and seizing ships/property outside the law
- **Mercenary** — a soldier fighting for pay rather than loyalty to a cause or country
- **Plenary powers** — full, complete authority to act on someone's behalf
- **Coahuila y Tejas** — the Mexican state that Texas was part of before independence; Texas had no separate state government of its own
- **Arbiter** — a judge or referee with final authority to decide something

## Three Sourcing Questions

1. The delegates who wrote this list of grievances were the same people declaring independence — there is no admission anywhere in the document of anything Texians did that provoked the Mexican government (for example, ignoring the 1830 immigration law, or the armed uprising at Anahuac in 1832). What does this one-sidedness tell you about the purpose of a declaration of independence as a *type* of document, compared to a neutral historical account of the same events?
2. Compare two grievances: "It has demanded the surrender of a number of our citizens, and ordered military detachments to seize and carry them into the Interior for trial" versus "It has, through its emissaries, incited the merciless savage, with the tomahawk and scalping knife, to massacre the inhabitants of our defenseless frontiers." Which of these two claims would be easier for a historian to check against other evidence (letters, military records, court documents), and which seems designed mainly to provoke an emotional reaction in the reader? What kind of evidence would you look for to test each one?
3. Two of the 59 signers — José Antonio Navarro and Francisco Ruiz — were Tejanos (Mexican-born residents of Texas), while most other signers were recent Anglo-American immigrants. Why might a Tejano's decision to sign this document, accusing his own national government of tyranny, carry different risk or meaning than the same decision made by a recent arrival from the United States? Does the presence of Navarro's and Ruiz's names make the document's grievances more representative of "Texas" as a whole, or should you be cautious about that conclusion?

## Teacher Note

**What's contested:** Historians agree the document served its purpose (winning outside sympathy and justifying the break), but they read individual grievances very differently. Some are well-corroborated by the documentary record — the 1834 dissolution and forced flight of the Coahuila y Texas state legislature, the imprisonment of political prisoners (this alludes to Stephen F. Austin's real imprisonment in Mexico City), and the demand that Texians surrender their arms. Others, especially "incited the merciless savage, with the tomahawk and scalping knife," reflect the racialized rhetoric of the era and are considered by historians to be exaggerated or unsubstantiated as a general Mexican government policy — this is explicitly flagged in the assignment as a good target for a sourcing exercise.

**What students commonly misread:** Students often assume all 59 signers, and Texans broadly, unanimously supported independence. In fact, a substantial "Peace Party" of Anglo settlers and many Tejano families either opposed independence or stayed neutral; most signers here were relatively recent Anglo-American arrivals rather than the earliest, most established colonists. Students also tend to treat every grievance as equally "true," rather than distinguishing verifiable administrative/legal complaints from more rhetorical, fear-based claims.

**The one thing worth saying out loud in class:** Many of these grievances — loyalty to the Constitution of 1824, anger at military power overriding civilian authority — are the *same complaints* Texians had been making since the Turtle Bayou Resolutions of 1832. What changed between 1832 and 1836 wasn't really the list of complaints; it was the conclusion drawn from them, from "we are loyal Mexican citizens opposing a bad administration" to "we are now our own nation." That shift is worth tracing explicitly if you're teaching both documents together.
