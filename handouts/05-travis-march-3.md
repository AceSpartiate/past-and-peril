# The Last Dispatch: Travis's Letter of March 3, 1836

**What this is:** William Barret Travis's last substantial letter out of the Alamo — an official military report to the President of the Convention, written three days after the famous "Victory or Death" letter and three days before the Alamo fell. It is less quoted than the February 24 letter, but far more detailed about the siege, and far more openly uncertain about whether help will come in time.

## Provenance Box

- **Author:** William Barret Travis, commander of the Alamo garrison
- **Date:** March 3, 1836
- **Place:** "Commandancy of the Alamo," Bejar (San Antonio de Béxar)
- **Audience:** Addressed formally to Richard Ellis, President of the Convention of 1836, then meeting at Washington-on-the-Brazos — this is an official report to the government, not a public appeal like the February 24 letter
- **How we have it:** No original manuscript of this letter is known to survive. A courier (accounts differ on whether it was William Bull or John W. Smith) carried it roughly 110 miles from the Alamo to Washington-on-the-Brazos, arriving the morning of Sunday, March 6, 1836 — the same day the Alamo fell, though the delegates would not learn that for days. Convention Secretary H. S. Kimble's own note, printed directly beneath the letter, records that it was read aloud to the assembled delegates that morning, that some members and citizens initially questioned whether the courier and the letter were genuine, and that the Convention then voted to have 1,000 copies printed for circulation. The text below comes from that printing: the *Telegraph and Texas Register* (San Felipe de Austin), Vol. 1, No. 19, March 12, 1836, page 3 — digitized by the Portal to Texas History. Because the original has never surfaced, this newspaper printing — checked by the Convention itself before it was set in type — is the earliest and most authoritative version of the text that exists today.

## The Text (verbatim, original spelling and punctuation preserved from the March 12, 1836 newspaper printing)

> FROM COL. TRAVIS TO THE PRESIDENT OF THE CONVENTION.
>
> Commandancy of the Alamo,
> Bejar, March 3, 1836.
>
> Sir, In the present confusion of the political authorities of the country, and in the absence of the commander-in-chief, I beg leave to communicate to you the situation of this garrison. You have doubtless already seen my official report of the action of the 25th ult., made on that day to Gen. Sam. Houston, together with the various communications heretofore sent by express. I shall therefore confine myself to what has transpired since that date.
>
> From the 25th to the present date, the enemy have kept up a bombardment from two howitzers, (one a five and a half inch, and the other an eight inch,) and a heavy cannonade from two long nine pounders, mounted on a battery on the opposite side of the river, at the distance of four hundred yards from our walls. During this period the enemy have been busily employed in encircling us with entrenched encampments on all sides, at the following distances, to wit, in Bejar, four hundred yards west; in Lavilleta, three hundred yards south; at the powder house, one thousand yards east by south; on the ditch, eight hundred yards north-east, and at the old mill, eight hundred yards north. Notwithstanding all this, a company of thirty-two men, from Gonzales, made their way into us on the morning of the 1st inst. at 3 o'clock, and Col. J. B. Bonham (a courier from Gonzales) got in this morning at 11 o'clock, without molestation.
>
> I have so fortified this place, that the walls are generally proof against cannon balls; and I still continue to entrench on the inside, and strengthen the walls by throwing up the dirt. At least two hundred shells have fallen inside of our works without having injured a single man: indeed, we have been so fortunate as not to lose a man from any cause, and we have killed many of the enemy. The spirits of my men are still high, although they have had much to depress them. We have contended for ten days against an enemy whose numbers are variously estimated at from fifteen hundred to six thousand men, with Gen. Ramirez Sesma and Col. Batres, the aid de camp of Santa Ana, at their head. A report was circulated that Santa Ana himself was with the enemy, but I think it was false. A reinforcement of about one thousand men is now entering Bejar from the west, and I think it more than probable that Santa Ana is now in town, from the rejoicing we hear. Col. Fannin is said to be on the march to this place with reinforcements; but I fear it is not true, as I have repeatedly sent to him for aid without receiving any. Colonel Bonham, my special messenger, arrived at La Bahia fourteen days ago, with a request for aid; and on the arrival of the enemy in Bejar ten days ago, I sent an express to Col. F., which arrived at Goliad on the next day, urging him to send us reinforcements — none have yet arrived. I look to the colonies alone for aid; unless it arrives soon, I shall have to fight the enemy on his own terms. I will, however, do the best I can under the circumstances; and I feel confident that the determined valor, and desperate courage, heretofore evinced by my men, will not fail them in the last struggle; and although they may be sacrificed to the vengeance of a gothic enemy, the victory will cost the enemy so dear, that it will be worse to him than a defeat.
>
> I hope your honorable body will hasten on reinforcements, ammunition, and provisions to our aid, as soon as possible. We have provisions for twenty days for the men we have: our supply of ammunition is limited. At least five hundred pounds of cannon powder, and two hundred rounds of six, nine, twelve, and eighteen pound balls, ten kegs of rifle powder, and a supply of lead, should be sent to this place without delay, under a sufficient guard. If these things are promptly sent, and large reinforcements are hastened to this frontier, this neighborhood will be the great and decisive battle-ground. The power of Santa Ana is to be met here, or in the colonies; we had better meet them here, than to suffer a war of desolation to rage in our settlements.
>
> A blood-red banner waves from the church of Bejar, and in the camp above us, in token that the war is one of vengeance against rebels; they have declared us as such, and demanded that we should surrender at discretion, or that this garrison should be put to the sword. Their threats have had no influence on me, or my men, but to make all fight with desperation, and that high souled courage which characterizes the patriot, who is willing to die in defence of his country's liberty and his own honor.
>
> The citizens of this municipality are all our enemies except those who have joined us heretofore; we have but three Mexicans now in the fort: those who have not joined us in this extremity, should be declared public enemies, and their property should aid in paying the expenses of the war.
>
> The bearer of this will give your honorable body a statement more in detail, should he escape through the enemies lines. God and Texas—Victory or Death!!
>
>           Your obedient ser't.
>           W. Barrett Travis.
>           Lieut. Col. Comm.
>
> P. S. The enemies troops are still arriving, and the reinforcement will probably amount to two or three thousand. T.

**The note the Convention's Secretary attached when the letter reached him, printed directly under the letter in the same newspaper:**

> Convention Hall, Sunday morning, 10 o'clock, March 6, 1836. On the arrival of the express with the letter, of which the above is a copy, the Convention was called together, and being called to order, on motion it was read; whereupon Mr. Parmer moved the house, that one thousand copies be printed for circulation, by the Editors, Messrs. Baker and Bordens of San Felipe, which was agreed.
>
>           H. S. Kimble, Secretary.

## Glossary

| Term | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| Commandancy | Military headquarters; the post a commander is in charge of |
| Ult. (as in "25th ult.") | Short for "ultimo" — "of last month" |
| Howitzer | A short cannon that fires shells in an arcing path, used against fortified positions |
| Cannonade | Sustained artillery fire |
| Entrenched encampments | Camps protected by dug trenches or earthworks |
| Aid de camp | A military officer serving as a personal assistant to a senior commander |
| Surrender at discretion | Surrendering completely, with no guaranteed terms or mercy — the attacker decides what happens to you |
| Municipality | A local governing district — here, the San Antonio/Bejar area |
| Express | A messenger or courier sent urgently, often on horseback |
| Garrison | The soldiers stationed to defend a fort |

## Three Sourcing Questions

1. This letter is addressed formally to "the President of the Convention" and reads like a military report — numbers, distances, supply requests — rather than the sweeping, publicly-aimed language of the February 24 letter. How does knowing the intended audience (a governing body deciding whether to send help, not "all Americans in the world") change how you should read Travis's tone and his choice of what details to include?
2. Convention Secretary Kimble's note records that when the letter arrived, "the veracity of the courier" and "the authenticity of the signature of Travis" were both questioned by some delegates before they accepted it as real. Why would people in 1836, receiving urgent news by messenger with no way to instantly verify it, be reasonably cautious about a document like this — and what does that caution tell you about how you should evaluate urgent, dramatic claims today?
3. No original manuscript of this letter exists — everything we have comes from a newspaper that printed it after the Convention itself reviewed and approved it for publication. Does that path (courier to Convention to newspaper to us) make this letter more or less trustworthy than a source that survives as a single original document, like the February 24 letter? What would a historian want to check before fully trusting either kind of source?

## Teacher Note

**What's contested:** The wording of the letter itself is well established — it comes from a newspaper printing that the Convention had already reviewed before authorizing for publication, and multiple independent modern transcriptions of that printing agree closely. What is genuinely contested by historians is which courier carried this exact letter (William Bull is documented delivering dispatches to the Convention on March 6; other accounts credit John W. Smith, who is well documented carrying earlier dispatches) — the Alamo's final days involved several couriers and several letters leaving on similar dates, and record-keeping in the chaos of the siege was imperfect. Present this letter as authentic and its content as reliable, while being honest that the courier's exact identity is one detail scholars still debate.

**What students commonly misread:** Because this letter is less famous than the February 24 "Victory or Death" letter, students sometimes assume it must be less important or less reliable. It is actually more detailed militarily and gives a much clearer, more grounded picture of the siege's final week — exact distances to enemy positions, exact supply counts, and Travis's own doubts about whether Fannin's reinforcements were real. Students should notice that Travis is far more guarded and specific here than in the public letter, because he is writing to people who could actually send help, not to history.

**Worth saying out loud:** By March 3, Travis already knew that repeated requests to Colonel Fannin at Goliad had produced no reinforcements, and he says so plainly ("I fear it is not true, as I have repeatedly sent to him for aid without receiving any"). This letter was written knowing rescue was uncertain at best — three days before the assault that killed him and every other defender who did not survive. It is worth pointing out to students that Travis did not know this would be his last letter to the Convention; he was still making requests for powder and lead as if resupply were possible.
