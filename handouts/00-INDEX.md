# PRIMARY SOURCE HANDOUTS — INDEX

Every handout follows the same seven-part structure: **what this is · provenance box · the text
(verbatim) · glossary · three sourcing questions · teacher note · citation and free URL.**

The sourcing questions target TEKS **7.20(D)** *(bias and point of view from historical context)*
and **7.20(F)** *(validity, credibility, bias, accuracy)* — the two standards a tabletop campaign
is best positioned to teach and the backbone of this unit's assessment.

---

## The rule these were built under

> **Never invent, reconstruct, or paraphrase-as-quotation a primary source.** Where wording could
> not be confirmed against a real archive, the handout says so in place of the text rather than
> filling the gap. **A fabricated primary source in a classroom is the one unrecoverable failure.**

Read the **Verification** column before you print. Two documents carry caveats, and both are worth
showing students *as* caveats — noticing that a famous quotation rests on a single 1928 translation
is exactly the skill 7.20(F) asks for.

## Two documents to handle with particular care

**07 — Santa Anna's execution order.** Urrea's diary, Portilla's diary, and Ramón Martínez Caro's
account (Santa Anna's *own secretary*) are verified verbatim. Santa Anna's dispatch itself is
quoted identically across independent sources but all trace to **Carlos Castañeda's 1928
translation**, and no scanned original was located. The file flags this in place. **If you want to
print that passage as verbatim, check it against a library copy of Castañeda's *The Mexican Side of
the Texas Revolution* first.** Everything else on the sheet stands.

**12 — half of it is deliberately unprintable, and that is the point.** **Part A** is Colonel Juan
Almonte's private journal, verified verbatim: his daily entries for Feb 27 – Mar 4, 1836, kept
outside the Alamo walls during the siege. They are almost entirely **weather readings.** Pair this
page with handout 04 and let students sit with it — *February 24th, Travis: "Victory or Death."
February 27th, a quarter mile away, Almonte: "Thermometer at 30 deg."* Nothing else in the unit
makes the point as economically, and Almonte's thermometer independently corroborates the cold snap
Texian accounts describe.

**Part B** is the de la Peña narrative — the most famous Mexican account, whose wording could not be
verified from any free archive (the 1975 translation is under copyright and behind paywalls). **No
text is reproduced.** Instead the page teaches the *authentication story*: the manuscript vanished
for a century, surfaced in 1955 via a coin dealer, sold at auction for $350,000, and was
forensically authenticated in 2001 by watermark and handwriting analysis — **and historians still
dispute one inserted paragraph**, the one claiming Crockett was executed after the battle. Every
fact in that story is verified. It is the cleanest demonstration in the whole set that *"the
document is real"* and *"every sentence in it is true"* are two different claims.

**Also note the provenance of Part A:** we can read Almonte's private diary only because Texians
captured it at San Jacinto and went through his baggage. He never consented and never knew.

**05 — Travis's March 3 letter has no surviving manuscript.** Only the newspaper printing exists
(*Telegraph and Texas Register*, March 12, 1836), which is what the handout cites. Which courier
carried it — William Bull or John W. Smith — is genuinely disputed. Both facts are flagged in the
file rather than smoothed over. **Compare this to handout 04, where the original paper survives.**
Two letters by the same man, eight days apart, with completely different evidentiary standing, is
the cleanest 7.20(F) lesson in the whole set.

## A note on the numbers at Goliad

Portilla's diary records **445 prisoners** assembled on the morning of March 27. The number
**executed** is usually given as **roughly 342**, with estimates from about 340 to over 400. The
gap is real — physicians, interpreters, and skilled workers were spared, some men were hidden by
**Francita Alavez**, and about **28 escaped**.

Show students the discrepancy rather than smoothing it. It is a concrete, one-minute lesson in why
careful historians write *approximately*, and why a source giving one confident number with no
range deserves a second look.
