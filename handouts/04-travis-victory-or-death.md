# "Victory or Death": Travis's Letter from the Alamo

**What this is:** The most famous letter of the Texas Revolution — Lieutenant Colonel William Barret Travis's appeal for help, written from inside the besieged Alamo, two days into the thirteen-day siege that ended in the deaths of every defender.

## Provenance Box

- **Author:** William Barret Travis, commander of the Alamo garrison
- **Date:** February 24, 1836
- **Place:** "Commandancy of the Alamo," Bejar (San Antonio de Béxar)
- **Audience:** Addressed broadly "To the People of Texas & all Americans in the world" — meant to be copied and carried to every settlement and printed in newspapers, not sent to one specific person
- **How we have it:** The **original manuscript**, in Travis's own hand. Captain Albert Martin carried it out of the Alamo on February 25 and passed it to Lancelot Smither, who delivered it to San Felipe within about 40 hours; it was copied and printed in Texas newspapers by March 2, 1836. The original letter passed down through Travis's family — his great-grandson John G. Davidson sold it to the State of Texas in 1893 for $85 — and it is now held by the Texas State Library and Archives Commission (TSLAC) in Austin, where the physical document can still be viewed.

## The Text (full text, verbatim — original spelling, punctuation, and line breaks preserved from the TSLAC transcription)

> Commandancy of the Alamo—
> Bejar, Fby. 24th 1836—
>
> To the People of Texas &
> all Americans in the world—
>
> Fellow citizens & compatriots—
> I am besieged, by a thousand
> or more of the Mexicans under
> Santa Anna—I have sustained
> a continual Bombardment &
> cannonade for 24 hours & have
> not lost a man—The enemy
> has demanded a surrender at
> discretion, otherwise, the garrison
> are to be put to the sword, if
> the fort is taken—I have answered
> the demand with a cannon
> shot, & our flag still waves
> proudly from the walls—I
> shall never surrender or retreat
> Then, I call on you in the
> name of Liberty, of patriotism &
> & every thing dear to the American
> character, to come to our aid,
> with all dispatch—The enemy is
> receiving reinforcements daily &
> will no doubt increase to three or
> four thousand in four or five days.
> If this call is neglected, I am deter
> mined to sustain myself as long as
> possible & die like a soldier
> who never forgets what is due to
> his own honor & that of his
> country—**Victory or Death**
>
>           William Barret Travis
>            Lt. Col. comdt
>
> P.S. The Lord is on our side—
> When the enemy appeared in sight
> we had not three bushels of corn—
> We have since found in deserted
> houses 80 or 90 bushels & got into
> the walls 20 or 30 head of Beeves—
>
>           Travis

**Two more hands wrote on the same document before it reached San Felipe.** These are not Travis's words, but they traveled with his letter and are part of the same physical artifact:

> Since the above was written I heard a very heavy Cannonade during the whole day think there must have been an attack made upon the alamo We were short of ammunition when I left Hurry on all the men you can in haste When I left there was but 150 determined to do or die tomorrow I leave for Bejar with what men I can raise [copy here illegible] at all events—Col. Almonte is there the troops are under the Command of Gen. Seisma
>
>           Albert Martin
>
> [printed sideways] N.B. I hope that Every One will Rondevu at gonzales as soon poseble as the Brave Solders are suffering do not neglect this powder is very scarce and should not be delad one moment
>
>           L. Smither

## Glossary

| Term | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| Commandancy | Military headquarters; the post a commander is in charge of |
| Besieged | Surrounded by enemy forces and cut off from escape or supply |
| Bombardment / cannonade | Sustained attack by artillery (cannon fire) |
| Surrender at discretion | Surrendering completely, with no guaranteed terms or mercy — the attacker decides what happens to you |
| Garrison | The soldiers stationed to defend a fort |
| Dispatch (here: "with all dispatch") | Speed; urgency — "as fast as possible" |
| Reinforcements | Additional troops sent to strengthen a military force |
| Compatriots | Fellow countrymen |
| Beeves | An old plural of "beef" — cattle |
| Rendezvous ("Rondevu") | A planned meeting point |

## Three Sourcing Questions

1. Travis addressed this letter "To the People of Texas & all Americans in the world," not to a specific commander or official. Why might he have chosen such a broad audience, and how does that choice shape the tone and content of what he wrote — knowing many strangers, and possibly history itself, would read it?
2. Travis writes "our flag still waves proudly from the walls" and "victory or death" while also privately facing very long odds (heavily outnumbered, with reinforcements uncertain). What is the difference between this letter as a factual military report and this letter as a piece of persuasion meant to inspire action? Find one phrase that seems aimed more at inspiring readers than simply informing them.
3. This letter survives as the actual original manuscript, but it was also hand-copied and reprinted in newspapers within days. If you were a historian in 1836 relying on a newspaper copy instead of the original, what kinds of small errors or changes might have crept in — and why does it matter that TSLAC holds the original today?

## Teacher Note

**What's contested:** Historians agree on the wording of this letter (the original survives), but they still debate *why* Travis wrote it the way he did — how much was genuine desperation versus a conscious appeal to history and the American public. Travis had reason to think about how he would be remembered: he was a former Alabama newspaper editor, and this is not his only letter written for a wide audience. Point this out explicitly — Travis was, in a real sense, writing for posterity as much as for reinforcements, and that self-awareness is part of why the letter reads the way it does.

**What students commonly misread:** Students often assume "victory or death" means Travis expected to die, full stop. In context, he is making a public commitment (and a plea) — he did not yet know reinforcements wouldn't come, and the line is doing rhetorical work (rallying help) as much as stating a private belief.

**Worth saying out loud:** Travis was a slaveholder, and he brought an enslaved man he held in bondage, Joe, with him to the Alamo. Joe survived the final assault on March 6 and is one of the few eyewitnesses to what happened inside the walls — his account, given afterward, is a separate primary source worth mentioning when this topic comes up, because it complicates the "everyone died, no one lived to tell it" version of the Alamo story that this letter alone might suggest.
