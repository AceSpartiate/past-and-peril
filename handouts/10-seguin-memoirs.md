# Handout 10: Juan N. Seguín's Personal Memoirs — "A Foreigner in My Native Land"

**What this is:** A passage from the memoir a Tejano hero of the Texas Revolution wrote in 1858 to defend his name, describing how he came to flee the country he had helped win independence for.

---

## Provenance

| | |
|---|---|
| **Author** | Juan Nepomuceno Seguín (1806–1890), Tejano (Texan of Mexican descent) military and political leader, born in San Antonio de Béxar under Spanish rule |
| **Date written/published** | 1858 — sixteen years after the events he is describing (his forced flight from Texas in 1842) |
| **Place** | Published in San Antonio, Texas |
| **Audience** | The general American and Texan public — Seguín published this memoir specifically to answer accusations of treason and disloyalty that had followed him for years, and to defend his reputation and legacy |
| **How we have it** | A printed pamphlet/booklet, "Personal Memoirs of John N. Seguin, From the Year 1834 to the Retreat of General Woll from the City of San Antonio in 1842," printed at the Ledger Book and Job Office, San Antonio, 1858. Seguín had fought for Texas independence at the Siege of Béxar and the Battle of San Jacinto, then served as a Texas senator — but by the early 1840s, rising anti-Mexican suspicion, threats on his life, and a false accusation that he had betrayed Texas to Mexico drove him into exile. This memoir is his own later account, written specifically to set the record straight in his own words. |

---

## The text

*(Quoted exactly as it appears in the excerpted passage; this is Seguín's own English-language published text, not a modern paraphrase.)*

> A victim to the wickedness of a few men, whose imposture was favored by their origin, and recent domination over the country; a foreigner in my native land; could I be expected stoically to endure their outrages and insults?
>
> Crushed by sorrow, convinced that my death alone would satisfy my enemies, I sought for a shelter amongst those against whom I had fought; I separated from my country, parents, family, relatives and friends, and what was more, from the institutions, on behalf of which I had drawn my sword, with an earnest wish to see Texas free and happy.

**[Note on wording: Seguín's own sentence reads "a foreigner in my native land" — not "a foreigner in my own land." The phrase "in my own land" is a popular paraphrase historians and textbooks often use as a title/summary of this passage, but it is not Seguín's exact wording. Both versions should not be treated as interchangeable direct quotations.]**

---

## Glossary

| Word/Term | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **Tejano** | A Texan of Mexican or Spanish descent, especially one whose family lived in Texas before it became part of the United States |
| **Imposture** | Deceit; pretending to be something you're not, or falsely accusing someone |
| **Origin** | Where someone comes from / their ancestry — here, referring to the Anglo-American background of Seguín's accusers |
| **Domination** | Control or power over something, especially by force |
| **Stoically** | Enduring pain or hardship without complaining |
| **Outrages** | Extremely offensive, shocking acts against someone |
| **Retreat of General Woll** | Refers to a brief 1842 Mexican military raid on San Antonio led by General Adrián Woll — the memoir's subtitle uses this as an ending point for the events it covers |
| **Institutions** | Here, the government, laws, and system Seguín fought to establish for the Republic of Texas |

## Three sourcing questions

1. Seguín wrote this memoir in 1858 — sixteen years after he fled Texas, and after years of being accused of treason by people in his own community. Why might a memoir written that long after the events, by someone still trying to clear his name, still count as valuable historical evidence, even though (or maybe *because*) he clearly has a personal stake in how the story is told?

2. Seguín fought for Texas independence at San Jacinto and served as a Texas senator, yet by 1842 he says he was treated as "a foreigner" in the country he helped create. What does the gap between how he saw his own contribution and how his neighbors treated him tell you about who got to count as a "real Texan" after independence?

3. This memoir was written specifically to defend Seguín's reputation, in English, for an American/Texan reading public — not as a private diary. How might knowing the *purpose* of a document (to persuade an audience, rather than just to record events) change how carefully you check its claims against other evidence?

## Teacher note

**What's contested:** Some of Seguín's contemporaries insisted he had actually sided with Mexico during the Woll raid; Seguín's memoir is his direct rebuttal to that charge, and most modern historians treat the accusations against him as products of anti-Mexican prejudice rather than solid evidence of treason — but that historical judgment is itself informed heavily by weighing Seguín's own testimony.

**What students commonly misread:** Students often assume Seguín "left" Texas the way a modern person moves — voluntarily, for convenience. His own words ("crushed by sorrow," "my death alone would satisfy my enemies") make clear this was a threatened man fleeing for his life, not a simple relocation, and that he saw it as being cut off from a nation he had helped build.

**Worth saying out loud:** The famous phrase students may have heard — "a foreigner in my own land" — is a paraphrase of Seguín's actual sentence, "a foreigner in my native land." It's a small wording difference, but it's a good, concrete example of why you always go back to what a person actually wrote rather than trusting a quote you've only seen secondhand.
