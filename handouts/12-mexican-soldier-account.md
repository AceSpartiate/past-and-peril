# Handout 12: The Other Side — A Mexican Officer's Journal

**What this is:** Two documents from the Mexican army, presented for two different reasons.
**Part A** is a Mexican colonel's private daily journal, kept *during the siege of the Alamo* — verified, printable, and startling in its plainness. **Part B** is the most famous Mexican account of the campaign, which you should **not** print, because we could not verify its wording — and the reason why is itself the best lesson in this entire handout set.

# PART A — The Private Journal of Juan N. Almonte
### Verified verbatim · printable · use this one in class

## Provenance Box

| | |
|---|---|
| **Author** | **Colonel Juan Nepomuceno Almonte**, aide and special advisor to General Santa Anna. Educated in the United States, fluent in English, and one of the most sophisticated men in the Mexican army. |
| **Dates kept** | February 1 – April 16, 1836 |
| **Place** | On campaign — the march north, the siege of Béxar, and the retreat toward San Jacinto |
| **Audience** | **Nobody.** This is a private journal. Almonte was not writing for publication, for history, or for us. |
| **How we have it** | **The Texians captured it.** Almonte's journal was taken at the Battle of San Jacinto on April 21, 1836, along with Almonte himself. It was carried off, printed in the *New York Herald*, and reprinted in the Texas press that autumn. It was later translated by Samuel E. Asbury and published as "The Private Journal of Juan Nepomuceno Almonte" in the *Southwestern Historical Quarterly*, Vol. 48 (July 1944). |

> **Say this to students before they read a word of it.** We can read a Mexican officer's private diary
> *only because his side lost a battle and his enemies went through his baggage.* Almonte never
> consented to this and never knew we would read it.
>
> **Ask them what that does to a source.** A private diary is unusually honest — the author has no
> reason to perform. And we hold it only through an act of war. Both of those things are true at
> once, and noticing that is TEKS **7.20(F)**.

## The Text (verbatim)

These are Almonte's complete entries for **February 27 through March 4, 1836** — the middle days of
the Alamo siege. He is camped outside the walls. Travis is inside, roughly a quarter mile away,
writing letters begging for help.

> **Saturday 27th** — The northern wind was strong at day break, and continued all the night.
> Thermometer at 30 deg.
>
> **Sunday 28th** — The weather abated somewhat. Thermometer at 40 deg. at 7 A. M.
>
> **Monday 29th** — The weather changed thermometer at 50 deg.; in the night it commenced blowing
> hard from the west.
>
> **March 1st** — The winds subsided, but the weather continued cold thermometer at 30 deg. in the
> morning day clear.
>
> **Wednesday 2d** — Commenced clear and pleasant thermometer 34 deg. no wind.
>
> **Thursday 3d** — Commenced clear, at 40 deg., without wind.
>
> **Friday 4th** — The day commenced windy, but not cold thermometer 48 deg.

*[Almonte's entries in this installment consist chiefly of weather observations; longer passages elsewhere in the journal record the council of war at which Generals Cos, Castrillón and others argued over whether to wait for the heavy artillery to arrive or to storm the walls immediately. Nothing has been altered. The 1836 newspaper printing is a noisy scan; the date "28th" is partly obscured by a printing flaw, but the sequence of days of the week is internally consistent and 1836 was a leap year, which is why February 29th exists.]*

---

## Glossary

- **Thermometer at 30 deg.** — 30° Fahrenheit. Below freezing.
- **Northern / norther** — a sudden cold wind out of the north; a Texas norther can drop the temperature 30 degrees in an hour
- **Abated** — died down, weakened
- **Subsided** — settled, calmed
- **Council of war** — a meeting where senior officers debate what the army should do next
- **Castrillón** — General Manuel Fernández Castrillón, who argued *against* storming the walls
- **Aide / advisor** — an officer who serves a general personally, carrying orders and giving counsel

---

## Three Sourcing Questions

1. Almonte was standing outside the Alamo for every day of this entry, and what he chose to write
   down was **the temperature.** Travis, inside the same walls on the same days, wrote *"I shall
   never surrender or retreat."* **What does the difference between those two documents tell you —
   about the two men, and about what each one was writing for?**

2. This journal was **captured**, not published by its author. Does a diary the writer never meant
   anyone to see make a *better* source than a letter written to be read by the whole world
   (handout 04)? Give one reason it might be better and one reason it might be worse.

3. Almonte does not describe suffering, glory, or the enemy. He records numbers. **Does that make
   him cold — or does it tell you something about what a professional officer's job actually was?**
   What would you need to find out before you decided?

## Teacher Note

**The single thing worth saying out loud:** hand students handout 04 and this page side by side and
let them sit with it. Those are the same days. **February 24th, Travis: "Victory or Death."
February 27th, a quarter mile away, Almonte: "Thermometer at 30 deg."**

Nothing else in this unit makes the point as economically. Both men are telling the truth. Neither
is writing for us. The Alamo has two documentary records running in parallel, and one of them is a
weather log.

**What students commonly misread:** they expect a Mexican account to be villainous, boastful, or
cruel, because that is how the other side is usually drawn. Almonte is *boring*, and the boredom is
the lesson — this was a professional army doing a job in bad weather. The cold is also corroboration:
Almonte's thermometer independently confirms the cold snap that Texian accounts of the siege
describe.

**What is contested:** very little here. The weather entries are as close to uncontested as a
primary source gets. The interpretive fights over the Alamo are about the assault and its
aftermath, not about the temperature.

**Citation:** "The Private Journal of Juan Nepomuceno Almonte, February 1 – April 16, 1836," trans.
Samuel E. Asbury, *Southwestern Historical Quarterly* 48, no. 1 (July 1944): 10–32. Entries above
transcribed from the 1836 newspaper printing: *Telegraph and Texas Register* (Columbia, Tex.), Vol.
1, No. 32, October 4, 1836, p. 1, reprinted from the *New York Herald*.
**Free:** https://texashistory.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metapth47894/m1/1/

# PART B — The de la Peña Narrative
### ⚠️ TEXT NOT VERIFIED — do not print the passage. Teach the controversy instead.

## Why this half exists

The most famous Mexican account of the campaign is the narrative of **José Enrique de la Peña**, a
lieutenant colonel of engineers with the elite Zapadores battalion on Santa Anna's staff.

**We could not verify any passage of it word-for-word from a free archive.** The 1975 translation is
under copyright and behind paywalls, bot-checks, and snippet-view restrictions. Per this unit's
rule, **no text is reproduced here.** A teacher who wants the real passage should obtain Carmen
Perry's translation (below) from a library.

**But do not skip this page.** The story of *how historians decided whether this document is real*
is the best available lesson in TEKS 7.20(F), and every fact in it is independently verified.

## The authentication story — all of this is confirmed

1. The manuscript **disappeared for over a century**, then surfaced in **1955** in the hands of a
   Mexican coin dealer. Published in Spanish that year as *La Rebelión de Texas*.
2. **Carmen Perry** translated it into English in **1975** as *With Santa Anna in Texas*. An
   expanded edition with a critical introduction by historian **James E. Crisp** followed in 1997.
3. The physical manuscript sat at the **John Peace Library, UT San Antonio** for about 25 years,
   was **sold at auction in 1998 for $350,000**, and is now in the **José Enrique de la Peña
   Collection at the Dolph Briscoe Center for American History, UT Austin.**
4. In **2001**, archivist **David B. Gracy II** published a forensic study — examining the paper,
   the ink, the **watermarks** (matching Mexican-army-era paper milled in Lisbon between 1825 and
   1832), and the handwriting against documents known to be de la Peña's — and concluded the
   manuscript is **authentic.**
5. **And that did not end the argument.** The single most famous passage in the document — the claim
   that **David Crockett survived the battle and was executed afterward** on Santa Anna's order —
   sits on a **physically separate leaf of paper inserted into the manuscript.** Historians **Bill
   Groneman** and **Thomas Ricks Lindley** have continued to argue that this specific passage may
   not be original to de la Peña. Crisp and others disagree.

## The three sourcing questions for Part B

1. Gracy tested the **paper, ink, watermarks, and handwriting** — not the story. **Why would a
   historian check the physical object before checking whether the events described are true?**
2. Almonte's journal (Part A) is a private diary written in the field. De la Peña's narrative is
   campaign notes that he **later rewrote and expanded**, apparently intending to publish a critical
   review of how Santa Anna ran the campaign. **Which of those two is more likely to be shaped by
   what the author wanted readers to believe?**
3. The manuscript is authentic **and** one paragraph inside it is disputed. **Explain in your own
   words why "the document is real" and "every sentence in it is true" are two different claims.**

## Teacher Note

**Say this out loud:** *"Historians spent forty-five years arguing about this, they used chemistry
and handwriting analysis to settle part of it, and they still disagree about one paragraph. That is
not historians failing. That is historians working."*

Students arrive believing history is a list of settled facts. This page is the cleanest available
demonstration that it is an ongoing argument conducted under rules — and that the rules are
strict enough that a $350,000 manuscript still doesn't get to claim more than it can prove.

**Citation:** José Enrique de la Peña, *With Santa Anna in Texas: A Personal Narrative of the
Revolution*, trans. Carmen Perry, expanded ed. with introduction by James E. Crisp (College Station:
Texas A&M University Press, 1997). Manuscript: José Enrique de la Peña Collection, Dolph Briscoe
Center for American History, University of Texas at Austin.
