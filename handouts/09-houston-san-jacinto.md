# Handout 9: Sam Houston's Official Report of the Battle of San Jacinto

**What this is:** The official military report General Sam Houston sent to the president of Texas four days after the battle that won Texas independence, describing the attack and its results in his own words.

---

## Provenance

| | |
|---|---|
| **Author** | Samuel (Sam) Houston, Commander-in-Chief of the Army of Texas |
| **Date written** | April 25, 1836 (four days after the battle of April 21, 1836) |
| **Place** | Written from the "Head Quarters of the Army," at the San Jacinto battleground (near present-day La Porte/Houston, Texas), where the army was still camped |
| **Audience** | Addressed to "His Excellency D. G. Burnet, President of the Republic of Texas" — Houston's commander-in-chief, David G. Burnet, head of the interim government |
| **How we have it** | This is an official government report, later copied/transcribed for the archives of the Republic of Texas. The version below is transcribed from the copy held by the Texas State Library and Archives Commission (TSLAC), cross-checked against a second transcribed copy (dated May 5, 1836) held by the University of North Texas's Portal to Texas History. Houston himself explains the four-day delay: he had been shot in the ankle during the battle and was in poor condition to write. |

---

## The text

*(Spelling, punctuation, and capitalization are exactly as transcribed from the original — Houston wrote in haste, from a wound, without time to polish his prose.)*

> Head Quarters of the army
> San Jacinto 25th April 1836
>
> To his Excellency D.G. Burnett
> President of the Republic of Texas
>
> Sir, I regret extremely that my situation since the Battle of the 21st has been such as to prevent my rendering you my official report of the same previous to this time.
>
> I have the honor to inform you that on the evening of the 18th Inst. after a forced march of fifty five miles, which was effected in two days and a half, the army arrived opposite Harrisburg...

**[... cut: several paragraphs describing the army's night march, the crossing of Buffalo Bayou, the pursuit of Santa Anna's forces toward Lynch's Ferry, a cavalry skirmish on April 20th, and the two armies taking up positions about three-quarters of a mile apart along the San Jacinto River — included here only in summary because it is mostly troop-movement detail, not needed to understand the attack itself ...]**

> About 9 o'clock on the morning of the 21st the Enemy were reinforced by 500 choice troops under the command of Gen. Cos, increasing their effective force to upwards of 1500 men, while our aggregate force for the field numbered 783...

**[... cut: Houston's description of forming his regiments and giving the order to advance at 3:30 p.m. — cut for length ...]**

> The Artillery advanced and took station within two hundred yards of the Enemy's Breastwork and commenced an effective fire with grape and cannister.

> ...the whole line at the center and on the right, advancing in double quick time, sung the war cry "Remember the Alamo" received the Enemy's fire and advanced within point blank shot before a piece was discharged from our lines—

> Our artillery having gallantly charged up within 70 yards of the Enemy's cannon, where it was taken by our troops.

> The conflict lasted about 18 minutes from the time of close action, until we were in possession of the Enemy's [encampment]...

**[... cut: details of the pursuit of fleeing Mexican soldiers ...]**

> In the Battle our loss was two killed and twenty three wounded, six of whom mortally.

> The Enemy's loss was 630 killed, among which was one General Officer, 4 Cols. 2 Liet. Cols. 5 Captains—12 Lieuts.

> President General Santa Anna, Gen. Cos, 4 Cols. aids to Gen Santa Anna, 6 Lieut. cols. the private Secretary of Gen Santa Anna, and the Col. of the Guerrero batallion are included in the number.
>
> *(This sentence follows Houston's statement, in the cut material just before it, that 730 prisoners were taken.)*

> About 600 muskets—300 Sabres, and 200 pistols have been collected since the action. Several hundred mules and Horses were taken, and near twelve thousand dollars in specie—

**[... cut: Houston's account of his own wound and praise for individual officers by name ...]**

> ...roads, illy supplied with rations and clothing, yet amid every difficulty they bore up with cheerfulness and fortitude, and performed their marches with spirit and alacrity - there was no murmuring.

> Our success in the action is conclusive proof of their daring intrepidity and valor; every officer and man proved himself worthy of the cause in which he battled, while the triumph received a luster from the humanity which characterized their conduct, after victory, and richly entitles them to the admiration and gratitude of their General.

> Nor should we withhold the tribute of our grateful thanks, from that Being who rules the destinies of nations and has in the time of [greatest] need enabled us to arrest a powerful invader whilst devastating our Country.

*Note: "[encampment]" and "[greatest]" are bracketed in the TSLAC source transcript itself, not added by us — the transcriber marked these as their best reading of a hard-to-read word in the original manuscript.*

## Glossary

| Word/Term | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **Inst.** | Short for "instant" — old way of saying "of this month" |
| **Forced march** | Marching an army very fast and far, pushing soldiers to their limit |
| **Breastwork** | A low, quickly-built wall (here, made of pack saddles and baggage) soldiers hide behind and fight from |
| **Grape and cannister (canister)** | Types of ammunition fired from a cannon — instead of one big ball, they scatter many small metal balls, like a giant shotgun blast |
| **Reconnoitering / reconnaissance** | Scouting ahead to see where the enemy is before fighting |
| **Aggregate force** | Total number of soldiers, added all together |
| **Specie** | Actual metal coin money (gold or silver), as opposed to paper money |
| **Intrepidity** | Fearlessness; boldness in danger |
| **"Remember the Alamo"** | The battle cry Texian soldiers shouted while charging — a reminder of the Texians killed at the Alamo six weeks earlier |

## Three sourcing questions

1. Houston wrote this report to his commanding officer (President Burnet) just four days after leading the battle himself, while recovering from a gunshot wound to the ankle. How might writing about your *own* victory, to your *own* boss, while still in pain, shape which details a person includes, exaggerates, or leaves out?

2. Houston reports enemy losses in precise numbers ("630 killed," "730" captured, specific officer counts) but is vaguer about how those numbers were counted, since the battle turned into a rout with Texians pursuing and killing fleeing Mexican soldiers for some time afterward. Why might exact-sounding numbers in a report like this still deserve a skeptical second look?

3. This report was Houston's only official, on-the-record account of the battle that made him a national hero and helped make him president of the new Republic. What personal and political reasons might Houston have had to describe the battle, his own leadership, and his soldiers exactly the way he did?

## Teacher note

**What's contested:** Historians still debate exactly how many Mexican soldiers died and how many were killed *after* they had already surrendered or were fleeing — the "18 minutes" of formal battle turned into a much longer and bloodier pursuit that Houston's report treats briefly. Some later Texian accounts (and some Mexican accounts) describe the aftermath as closer to a massacre than a battle.

**What students commonly misread:** Students often assume "630 killed" vs. "two killed" means the battle itself was one-sided and quick from start to finish. It's worth pointing out that the *initial* 18-minute engagement was followed by hours of one-sided killing of fleeing men — the casualty gap reflects that, not just superior Texian marksmanship in the initial charge.

**Worth saying out loud:** Houston is not a neutral narrator describing someone else's battle — he is the winning general, writing to his own president, about his own command decisions, four days after the fact. That doesn't make the report false, but every number and every claim of credit in it comes from the man with the most to gain from how the story gets told.
