# Handout 2: The Law of April 6, 1830 (selected articles)

**What this is:** A Mexican federal law passed by the national Congress in Mexico City that tried to stop further U.S. immigration into Texas, tightened control over the colonies, and restricted slavery's growth — one of the biggest single causes of Anglo-Texan anger in the years before the Revolution.

---

## Provenance Box

| | |
|---|---|
| **Author** | The Mexican federal Congress, under President Anastasio Bustamante, driven largely by Minister of Relations **Lucas Alamán**. Alamán based much of the law on a report by **General Manuel de Mier y Terán**, who had toured Texas in 1828 and warned his government that Anglo-American settlers already outnumbered Mexican citizens there. |
| **Date** | April 6, 1830 |
| **Place** | Mexico City (the seat of the Mexican federal government) |

## The Text — selected articles, verbatim (English translation)

This law had 18 articles. Most (covering money for transportation, customs duty schedules, and fortification budgets) are financial/administrative and are omitted here. The four articles below are the ones historians identify as the most consequential and the most commonly confused with one another — read them carefully, because they do **different** things.

> **Article 3.** The government is authorized to name one or more commissioners who shall visit the colonies of the frontier states and contract with the legislatures of said states for the purchase, in behalf of the Federal government, of lands deemed suitable for the establishment of colonies of Mexicans and other nationalities; and the said commissioners shall make, with the existing colonies, whatever arrangements seem expedient for the security of the republic. The said commissioners shall supervise the introduction of new colonists and the fulfilling of their contract for settlement, and shall ascertain to what extent the existing contracts have been completed.

> **Article 9.** The introduction of foreigners across the northern frontier is prohibited under any pretext whatsoever, unless the said foreigners are provided with a passport issued by the agent of the republic at the point whence the said foreigners set out.

> **Article 10.** No change shall be made with respect to the slaves now in the states, but the Federal government and the government of each state shall most strictly enforce the colonization laws, and prevent the further introduction of slaves.

> **Article 11.** In accordance with the right reserved by the general congress in the seventh article of the law of August 18, 1824, it is prohibited that emigrants from nations bordering on this republic shall settle in the states or territory adjacent to their own nation. Consequently, all contracts not already completed and not in harmony with this law are suspended.

**[...] — omitted here: Articles 1, 2, 4–8, and 12–18**, which cover cotton-goods import duties, reserve funds against a feared Spanish invasion, transporting and resettling convict-soldiers and their families as colonists, recruiting voluntary Mexican colonist families, coastwise trade privileges for foreign merchants, loan and customs financing arrangements, and a reporting requirement to Congress. These matter to economic and military historians but are not the provisions Texas colonists reacted to.

## Glossary

- **Empresario** — a person contracted by the Mexican government to bring a set number of families to settle a land grant in Texas (Stephen F. Austin was the most famous)
- **Colonization laws** — the set of Mexican laws (state and federal) governing how land was granted to settlers and how colonies were supervised
- **Commissioner** — a government official appointed to inspect, supervise, or negotiate on behalf of the federal government
- **Frontier** — the northern border region of Mexico, including Texas, seen by Mexico City as vulnerable and hard to control
- **Passport** — official travel documentation; Article 9 required foreigners entering from the north to already have one before crossing
- **Federal government vs. state government** — under Mexico's 1824 Constitution, Texas was part of the state of Coahuila y Texas; this law shifted power over colonization from that state government to the national government in Mexico City
- **Decree** — an official order issued by a government that has the force of law
- **Adjacent** — next to, or bordering (Article 11 restricts emigrants from nations "adjacent" to the Mexican territory they'd be settling)

## Three Sourcing Questions

1. Article 11 never names the United States — it bans emigrants from "nations bordering on this republic" from settling in "adjacent" Mexican territory. In 1830, the United States was the only bordering nation sending large numbers of settlers into Texas. Why might the law's authors have chosen this indirect, general wording instead of naming the U.S. directly? What does that phrasing choice tell you about how a government writes a law aimed at one group without saying so outright?
2. This law was shaped by a report from General Manuel de Mier y Terán, a Mexican official who had personally traveled through Texas and grown alarmed that Anglo-American colonists already outnumbered Mexican citizens there. How might Terán's job — defending and holding Mexico's northern frontier — have shaped which articles he recommended? How might an Anglo-Texan colonist reading Articles 9 and 11 in 1830 have interpreted the same policy very differently than Terán did?
3. Article 10 says "no change shall be made with respect to the slaves now in the states" while ordering government to "prevent the further introduction of slaves" — it does not free anyone already enslaved in Texas, only restricts bringing in more. Why might the Mexican government, which generally opposed slavery, have written the article this way instead of abolishing slavery in Texas outright? What does the exact wording tell you about the limits of this law's anti-slavery intent, compared to what people sometimes assume it did?

## Teacher Note

**What's contested / commonly confused:** Students (and popular retellings) very often flatten this law into "Mexico banned slavery and immigration in 1830," blending three *separate* articles into one. Article 9 requires a passport for foreigners entering from the north — a border-control measure, not a ban. Article 10 restricts *new* slave importation but explicitly protects existing slaveholding. Article 11 is the actual immigration ban, and it also suspended *incomplete* empresario contracts — importantly, Stephen F. Austin and Green DeWitt successfully got their own contracts exempted, so colonization under those two empresarios continued even after this law passed. Point this out explicitly: "Article 11" and "the law banned slavery" are two different, often-confused claims, and neither one alone tells the whole story.

**What's genuinely debated by historians:** How much this law actually stopped immigration versus how much it was evaded. Because Austin's and DeWitt's contracts were exempted, and because enforcement along such a long, thinly-guarded frontier was difficult, many settlers kept arriving illegally or through loopholes after 1830. Historians differ on whether the law's real historical importance was its *practical* effect (limited) or its *psychological/political* effect on Anglo colonists, who now saw clear proof that the Mexican government viewed them as a threat to be contained.

**The one thing worth saying out loud in class:** This law didn't stop the thing it was trying to stop — American immigration into Texas kept happening — but it succeeded completely at convincing Anglo-Texans that Mexico City saw them as a problem, not as citizens. That feeling of being treated as suspects rather than neighbors is what shows up two years later in the Turtle Bayou Resolutions and six years later in the Texas Declaration of Independence's list of grievances.
