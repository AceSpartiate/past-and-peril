# "You Make Your Mind Easy, Sir": A Survivor of the Goliad Massacre

**What this is:** The eyewitness account of Andrew A. Boyle, a Texian soldier who was wounded at the Battle of Coleto, imprisoned at Goliad, and lived through the March 27, 1836 massacre because a Mexican officer recognized his name.

---

## Provenance Box

- **Author/narrator:** Andrew A. Boyle (born Andrew O'Boyle), a private in Captain Ira Westover's artillery company, part of Col. James Fannin's command
- **Date:** The events described happened March 17–27, 1836. Boyle's account itself was recorded decades later, as part of his Republic of Texas pension application — a sworn statement veterans gave the Texas government to prove their service and qualify for a pension. (A closely related version of Boyle's recollections was also dictated in 1870, near the end of his life, to his daughter, and was later published in *Frontier Times* magazine in 1928 and reprinted in *True West* magazine in 1958.)
- **Place:** Fort Defiance (Goliad), and the battlefield at Coleto Creek nearby
- **Audience:** A government official processing his pension claim — this was a legal/official document, not something written for the public
- **How we have it:** The Texas State Library and Archives Commission holds and has transcribed Boyle's Republic Pension Application. Boyle's status as a Goliad survivor is independently confirmed by the Muster Rolls of the Texas Revolution, which list "Private A. M. O'Boyle" in Capt. Ira Westover's Company with the notation "escaped massacre."

**Why this matters for reading it carefully:** Boyle told this story roughly 35 years after it happened, as an old man near the end of his life, for the specific purpose of proving to the government that he deserved a pension. That does not make it false — his survival is confirmed by other records — but it means small details of memory (exact times, exact wording of what people said to him) had 35 years to soften or sharpen before they were written down.

## The Text (verbatim, spelling and punctuation as recorded)

*[Excerpted: the account also describes the Battle of Coleto Creek in detail over two full days before the section below. That earlier battle narrative is cut here to focus this handout on the surrender, imprisonment, and massacre. Cuts are marked with [...].]*

> [...] All of our men not wounded were marched back to Goliad and the wounded remained from two to three days for Mexican carts to carry them there, their sufferings were great from the heat of the sun, want of water and medical aid. The wounded upon their arrival at Goliad were put into an hospital, those not wounded were placed in a yard of the Fort with sentries around them.
>
> **The Massacre**
>
> One week from the day of capitulation all the men except the wounded were marched out of the Fort in separate divisions, taken a short distance and shot. A Mexican officer came into the hospital where I was and asked me to tell the men, he wanted all those who were able to walk to come outside, the men commenced gathering up their blankets, in the meantime four mexican soldiers came in and commenced carrying out those who were unable to walk. I was assisted out by two comrades who were slightly wounded, having the use of one leg only; I was informed by an officer at the door that we were all going to be shot, on getting outside I told the men. The wounded lay in the corner of the yard in which the church door fronts. A Company of Mexican infantry marched down and formed in front of us. I particularly noticed that they loaded with ball cartridge, a file of men under a Corporal took two of our men, marched them out towards the Company, bandaged their eyes made them lay face down-wards, pointed muskets to their heads and shot them.
>
> About this time a Mexican officer, seemingly of distinction came in among us and asked in a loud voice in English, "if there was any person there by the name of Boyle" I answered, (being close by him, leaning against the wall of the hospital on one leg) He told me "that he had been looking for me all the morning," he then called a Mexican officer and told him "to have me taken to the Mexican officer's hospital, to tell the Doctor to attend to my wound and he would be there after a while." [...]
>
> A few days after we had been taken prisoners, Col Ward and his men were brought into Goliad as prisoners, they having been captured as we understood some place between the Lavaca and Navidad Rivers. They were shot with our men. The number of men shot that morning according to the Mexican report was Four Hundred and Seventeen (417).
>
> **Rescue**
>
> About 2½ hours after my arrival at the Mexican officers hospital, the Mexican officer who ordered me in the morning to be sent there, came in. He addressed me in the English language saying; "You make your mind easy Sir, your life is spared." I asked him "if he would permit me to ask the name of the person to whom I was indebted for my life," he answered me "certainly, my name is General Francisco Garay, second in command of Urea's division," I then asked him "how it was that he happened to know my name," he told me "that when the division passed through San Patricio he had quarters at my Brother and sister's house and he was treated with a great deal of kindness by them... My sister refused to accept any remuneration, saying that she was most happy to have him there as his presence was a protection to the house, he then asked if he could do anything for her, she replied, there is one thing General you can do for me, which I will esteem as a great favor, I have a Brother in the Revolutionary Army and should he through the fortunes of war ever fall into your hands as a prisoner, see that he is well treated..." He told me that he was leaving that day to join Genl Urea but that he had instructed Col. Portillo who would remain in command of the garrison to give me a passport to go home whenever I called for it. A few days after I procured the Passport and went in a ox team to the Mission where I remained a short time. I started again in an ox cart for San Patricio where we arrived on the third day [...]

*[sic] — spelling of names like "Fanning" for Fannin, "Urea" for Urrea, and "Portillo" for Portilla, and archaic touches like "oclock," reflect Boyle's own usage and are preserved as recorded.*

## Glossary

- **Capitulation** — the terms of a formal surrender.
- **Ball cartridge** — real ammunition meant to kill, as opposed to blank cartridges — Boyle notices this detail specifically because it told him the executions were real, not a bluff.
- **File of men** — a small line/squad of soldiers.
- **"Of distinction"** — Boyle's way of saying the officer's uniform or bearing showed he was high-ranking.
- **Remuneration** — payment given in return for a service or kindness.
- **Passport** (as used here) — an official paper allowing safe travel through a war zone.
- **Muster roll** — an official military list of the soldiers in a unit.
- **Pension application** — a formal request to a government for a regular payment owed to a former soldier, usually requiring the veteran to prove their service.

## Three Sourcing Questions

1. Boyle's account was recorded decades after the massacre, as part of a request for a government pension. What specific reason might Boyle have had to make sure his story clearly proved he was really there and really wounded? Does this change how you'd weigh a very precise detail (like the exact words Garay spoke) versus a general fact (like being wounded at Coleto)?
2. Boyle says "the number of men shot that morning according to the Mexican report was Four Hundred and Seventeen (417)." Notice that he attributes this specific number to a Mexican report, not to his own count. Why might Boyle have been careful to say where that number came from, rather than just stating it as fact?
3. Boyle survived specifically because a Mexican officer, General Garay, recognized his name and repaid a personal debt of kindness. What does this detail suggest about how the massacre was carried out — was it a single uniform event, or did individual Mexican soldiers and officers make different choices? How might a survivor's account like Boyle's give us a different picture of the massacre than a document like Santa Anna's order would?

## Teacher Note

What's contested: Boyle's account is not seriously disputed as fabricated — his survival is independently confirmed by muster rolls — but historians do note that *all* Goliad survivor narratives were recorded well after the fact (Boyle's, decades later) and that the exact dialogue quoted in any of them (the precise words Garay or the Mexican officers used) should be read as Boyle's best memory of the gist of what was said, not a court-reporter transcript. This is true of nearly every eyewitness account of this event; there was no reporter standing in the yard with a notebook.

What students commonly misread: students often assume "eyewitness" means "recorded immediately." Point out the gap — the events were in 1836; Boyle's sworn pension narrative and his dictated 1870 recollections came 34+ years later. That gap is normal for how we have most Texas Revolution survivor testimony, and it's a feature to analyze, not a reason to dismiss the source.

The one thing worth saying out loud: the massacre was carried out by hundreds of individual soldiers following one order, but Boyle's life was saved by one individual officer's personal choice and a private family kindness from years before the war. Both things are true of Goliad at the same time — read this alongside the Santa Anna/Urrea orders handout (07) to see the same event from the "top" (the order) and here from inside the yard where it happened.
