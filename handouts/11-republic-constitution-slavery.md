# The Constitution Writes Slavery Into Law: General Provisions, 1836

**What this is:** Sections of the "General Provisions" of the Constitution of the Republic of Texas — the actual legal text, adopted by the same convention that had just declared independence, that protected slavery, barred the new Congress from ever abolishing it, and defined who could and could not be a citizen of the Republic on the basis of race.

## Provenance Box

- **Author/body:** Written and adopted by the delegates of the Convention of 1836, meeting at Washington-on-the-Brazos — the same convention that adopted the Texas Declaration of Independence on March 2
- **Date:** Adopted March 17, 1836 (the convention adjourned the next day, March 18, as Santa Anna's army advanced and the delegates fled east in what is called the Runaway Scrape)
- **Place:** Washington-on-the-Brazos, Texas
- **Audience:** This is not a letter or a speech — it is binding law, written to govern the new Republic of Texas and to be enforced by its courts, Congress, and citizens
- **How we have it:** The Constitution was recorded in the convention's official proceedings and printed for distribution. The transcription below comes from the Tarlton Law Library's digital collection of historic Texas constitutions, which preserves the original wording, spelling, and punctuation (including small inconsistencies) exactly as adopted in 1836.

## The Text (verbatim, original spelling and punctuation preserved)

**GENERAL PROVISIONS.**

**SEC. 9.** All persons of color who were slaves for life previous to their emigration to Texas, and who are now held in bondage, shall remain in the like state of servitude, provide the said slave shall be the bona fide property of the person so holding said slave as aforesaid. Congress shall pass no laws to prohibit emigrants from the United States of America from bringing their slaves into the Republic with them, and holding them by the same tenure by which such slaves were held in the United States; nor shall Congress have power to emancipate slaves; nor shall any slave-holder be allowed to emancipate his or her slave or slaves, without the consent of Congress, unless he or she shall send his or her slave or slaves without the limits of the Republic. No free person of African descent, either in whole or in part, shall be permitted to reside permanently in the Republic, without the consent of Congress, and the importation or admission of Africans or negroes into this Republic, excepting from the United States of America, is forever prohibited, and declared to be piracy.

**[...The Constitution addresses citizenship in two other sections of the same General Provisions. Both are given here in full because they define citizenship using the same racial line drawn in Section 9.]**

**SEC. 6.** All free white persons who shall emigrate to this Republic, and who shall, after a residence of six months, make oath before some competent authority that he intends to reside permanently in the same, and shall swear to support this Constitution, and that he will bear true allegiance to the Republic of Texas, shall be entitled to all the privileges of citizenship.

**SEC. 10.** All persons, (Africans, the descendants of Africans, and Indians excepted,) who were residing in Texas on the day of the Declaration of Independence, shall be considered citizens of the Republic, and entitled to all the privileges of such. **[...the rest of Section 10 continues for several more sentences about land grants — how much land a head of family or single man already living in Texas was entitled to receive. That land-grant language is not reproduced here because it does not bear on the citizenship question this handout is about.]**

*(Transcription note, from Tarlton Law Library: errors in the original document are preserved as written.)*

## Glossary

| Term | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| General Provisions | A section of a constitution holding rules that don't fit neatly into the articles about the president, Congress, or courts — a catch-all section, but still fully binding law |
| Servitude | The condition of being enslaved or bound to labor for someone else |
| Bona fide | Genuine, in good faith — here, meaning the enslaver had to actually, legally own the person as property |
| Emancipate | To free someone from slavery |
| Slave-holder | A person who legally claimed ownership of an enslaved person |
| Free person of African descent | A Black person who was not enslaved |
| Piracy | Robbery or violent crime committed at sea; here, the Constitution uses the word to describe importing enslaved Africans directly (rather than through the United States), making it a capital crime |
| Naturalization / citizenship | The legal status that gives a person full rights and protections under a country's government |
| Descendants | Children, grandchildren, and later generations of a person |

## Three Sourcing Questions

1. Section 9 was written by delegates who had just finished declaring, in the Texas Declaration of Independence, that Mexico had violated their "inalienable rights" and their right to "liberty." Whose liberty did this Constitution actually protect, and whose did it deny in the very next set of pages? What does the gap between those two documents tell you about whose freedom the delegates meant when they used the word?
2. This is not a letter, diary, or speech expressing someone's opinion — it is adopted law. Does that make it more or less reliable as evidence of what Texas actually did in 1836, compared to a source like a newspaper editorial from the same year? Explain your reasoning using the difference between a source telling you what someone *believed* versus what a government *did*.
3. Section 9 singles out enslaved people who arrived "previous to their emigration to Texas" and separately guarantees that future emigrants from the United States could keep bringing enslaved people with them. Why might the delegates have written the section this way instead of simply saying "slavery is legal here"? What does the specific, careful wording suggest about what they anticipated being challenged on?

## Teacher Note

**What's contested:** Nothing about the wording here is contested — this is adopted constitutional law, transcribed directly from the Tarlton Law Library's collection of historic Texas constitutions, and it says what it says. What historians do debate is how central the protection of slavery was to the motivations for the Texas Revolution as a whole, alongside other causes such as centralism, tariffs, and land disputes. This handout does not resolve that debate; it only puts the actual constitutional text in front of students so they can read the legal reality of 1836 for themselves, rather than being told about it secondhand.

**What students commonly misread:** Students sometimes assume Section 9 simply "allowed" slavery, similar to how it existed elsewhere. Point out the stronger, more specific language: Congress was constitutionally *forbidden* from ever abolishing it, and even an individual slaveholder could not free someone they enslaved without government permission (or by removing them from Texas entirely). This was not neutral permission — it was active, structural protection.

**Worth saying out loud:** Section 10 excludes "Africans, the descendants of Africans, and Indians" from citizenship in the same breath that it grants citizenship to nearly everyone else already living in Texas — a group that included Tejanos (Texas-born Mexicans) who fought for and signed the Declaration of Independence, such as José Antonio Navarro and José Francisco Ruiz. Citizenship in the new Republic was defined by race, in the plain text of the law, not by loyalty or service to Texas.
