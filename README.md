# PAST & PERIL
### Come and Take It — the Gonzales campaign
### A seven-session digital classroom RPG that teaches the Texas Revolution
**7th Grade Texas History · 19 TAC §113.19 (Adopted 2022) · ~30 students · one teacher · 45–50 minute periods · no prior gaming experience required**

**7 core sessions + 2 optional · ~45 min each · ~315 min of core instruction**

---

## What this is

A classroom roleplaying game, played on the students' own screens, in which your class becomes the town of **Gonzales, Texas, in
October 1835** — and lives through the Texas Revolution from the first shot to annexation.

Gonzales is not a generic setting. It was chosen because its real history is a complete tragic arc
that requires no invention:

> The war's first shots are fired **there**, over **their** cannon. The Gonzales Ranging Company
> rides to the Alamo and dies there to a man. Houston burns the town during the
> Runaway Scrape. And Gonzales survivors stand in the line at San Jacinto.

They are remembered as the "Immortal 32" — though that count was reconstructed later from
several imperfect lists, and appears whole in no 1836 document. The game says so out loud.

Your town starts the war, pays for it, burns, and wins it. **Every emotional beat is documented
fact.**

## The entire rule set

**Every turn, every student picks one verb and writes it on their Slate — all at the same time.**

> **ACT** — do your thing; you're in the roll · **AID** — name someone, they get +1 ·
> **GUARD** — take a hit meant for someone · **SEEK** — find out, always works ·
> **SPEAK** — argue publicly, costs a Word · **HOLD** — bank it, +1 Legacy

Then each company rolls once:

> **THE MUSTER.** Roll **2d6**, add your company's **Standing** (0–2), add **AIDs** (max 3), add
> **Words** spent (+1 each, or **+2 on your Strength — and you narrate**). Max three Words a roll.
>
> **10+** held · **7–9** held at a cost · **6 or less** gave ground — **and you still learn
> something true.**

Everyone then reads their own outcome off a printed table. **Six rolls, thirty personal outcomes.**

Teachable in 90 seconds. It fits on the Slate. There is no second page and no rulebook to buy.

## The three design decisions that make it work

### 1. The spotlight math forced the whole structure
Seven sessions × 47 minutes ÷ 30 students = **about ten minutes of individual attention per student
for the entire unit.** That arithmetic kills the default 30-person-party design outright. So play
resolves in **parallel companies**, not sequential turns, and every student holds a **role that is
the sole source of one kind of claim.** If they're absent, their company visibly loses that
capability, in the fiction, immediately.

### 1b. Nobody takes turns — everyone acts at once

The original design gave each student two guaranteed moments per session. That beat the arithmetic,
but it accepted a premise it didn't have to: *that students take turns.* They don't have to.

**Every turn, all thirty students write one of six verbs on a Slate, simultaneously, in the same 90
seconds:**

| **ACT** | **AID** | **GUARD** | **SEEK** | **SPEAK** | **HOLD** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Do your thing — you're in the roll, win *or* lose | Name someone; they get +1 | Take a hit meant for someone else | Find out — **always works** | Argue publicly; costs a Word | Bank it; clear Resolve, +1 Legacy |

Then six company rolls resolve at once and each student reads their own outcome off a printed table.
**One roll, thirty personal outcomes.** The teacher narrates two or three declarations per turn —
never thirty. That discipline is what keeps a turn at six minutes.

**The verbs are universal; the options inside them are not.** Every student also holds a **Calling
Card** — an 8×5 grid of their trade crossed with the five kinds of moment the campaign contains
(`COUNCIL · MARCH · SIEGE · BATTLE · AFTERMATH`), and every turn in every session is tagged with its
kind. So a Healer and a Rifleman at the same table, in the same Situation, are handed **visibly
different** things to do. In an AFTERMATH the Rifleman's card reads *stand down, publicly, where
others can see you do it.* The Healer's reads *treat the wounded on the other side.* Same verb,
utterly different act — and the difference came from who they chose to be on Muster Day.

**ACT is not the right answer**, which is the load-bearing point. A bad roll lands *only* on the
students who ACTed. Three students AIDing is +3 — the difference between a 7 and a 10. GUARD is the
bravest thing on the card and never looks like it. And SEEK can never fail, because *the facts are
free.*

| | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Declarations per student, per session | 2 | **5–6** |
| Active decision time, whole campaign | ~10 min | **~54 min** |
| Time waiting for a turn | most of the period | **~0** |
| Class-wide declarations, whole campaign | ~360 | **~1,080** |

Full engine and the honest tradeoffs — including the ELPS cost and how the **Reveal** beat repays it
— in [`design/05-simultaneous-turns.md`](design/05-simultaneous-turns.md).

### 2. The metric is the mechanic
Every student gets **2 Words** per session as physical tokens. Spending one costs a sentence of
historical reasoning — which is TEKS **7.20(E)** performed roughly sixty times per period.

Participation is **equal by construction**: the loudest student has the same two as the quietest,
and quiet students end the session holding the resource everyone needs, so *scarcity does the
inviting.*

And you never keep a tally sheet. **Count the tokens left on the table at the bell.** That is your
participation distribution and decision density, free.

### 2b. Students play real, named, documented people

**The record is fixed. The silence is yours.**

Every student is dealt a **Person Card** — an actual documented resident of Gonzales, the DeWitt
Colony, or the ranchos of the Guadalupe. Name, age, trade, family, and fate are locked. But the
record is mostly *silence*, and that silence is where the student builds:

| Step | Source | Grants |
|---|---|---|
| **Your Person** | Fixed by history | The name you'll answer to for six sessions |
| **Your Calling** *(the "class")* | Fixed if documented, chosen if not | +1 stat, and one ability per session |
| **Your Origin** | Fixed if documented, chosen if not | +1 stat |
| **Your Mark** | **Always the student's** | +1 stat — and a real, enforced liability |

Four stats — **Arms, Talk, Land, Word**. Eight Callings, all real 1835 occupations (Rifleman,
Rider, Smith, Trader, Clerk, Healer, Ranchero, Householder). **Your highest stat is your Strength**,
and a Word spent on your Strength is worth double *and lets you narrate the outcome* — narration
being the real prize at any table.

Every **Mark** costs something. Choose *Newly Arrived* and you came after the Law of April 6, 1830,
so your land title is legally void and you lose any property dispute automatically. That student has
now internalized TEKS 7.3(A) as a liability they carry for six sessions.

**One person, one player** — nobody shares a character, and a character who dies is retired
permanently. Their sheet goes on the Wall of the Sent and **their name comes off the roster.** You
cannot take a dead person's name.

**When a character dies before San Jacinto, the student builds a new one in three minutes** from the
**Latecomers** roster — the New Orleans Greys, the Alabama Red Rovers, refugees of the Scrape,
displaced Tejano federalists, and the free Black Texians who fought at Béxar and Goliad and then had
to petition Congress for permission to remain in the country they'd bled for. They don't have to be
from Gonzales. They just have to have walked into someone else's war.

Full system: [`design/04-character-creation.md`](design/04-character-creation.md).

### 3. Fixed history, open conduct
Two rules protect the teaching:

- **THE FACTS ARE FREE.** No historical content is ever gated behind a die roll. Dice govern cost
  and consequence, never access. A student who rolls badly all unit still learns all of it.
- **DICE NEVER KILL ANYONE.** Historical deaths are announced at the dramatically correct moment.
  No 12-year-old loses a character to bad luck.

This produces the campaign's structural spine — two mirrored sessions:

| | Session 3 — the Alamo | Session 5 — San Jacinto |
|---|---|---|
| The outcome | **You cannot win.** | **You cannot lose.** |
| The real question | *What will you leave behind?* | *What kind of people are you when you win?* |

Students are told both of these out loud, in advance. Removing the false hope is what makes them
land.

| 3 | [Two Clocks](gm-materials/session-3-two-clocks.md) | Feb 23 – Mar 6, 1836 | Who do we send — and what are we even fighting for? |
| 4 | [Remember Goliad](gm-materials/session-4-remember-goliad.md) | Mar 13 – Apr 15, 1836 | Our town is ash and 342 men were shot. What do we do with that? |

**Every session runs the same 45-minute skeleton** — Dispatch, cold open, Muster I, the Turn,
Muster II, Dispatch, Set the Record Straight. You learn the shape once.

**Session 3 requires the room reset into three tables before the bell.** It is the only session
with a physical setup requirement, and it will not work if you improvise it live.

## The four minutes you must never cut

Every session ends with **SET THE RECORD STRAIGHT** — a scripted four minutes where you name what
was real, what was invented, and what historians still argue about.

This is borrowed from *Reacting to the Past*, the longest-running historical-roleplay pedagogy in
American higher education, where the post-game correction is treated as non-optional. **A
simulation without it teaches students a feeling about history instead of history.** The script is
written out in every session file. Read it; don't improvise it.

## Content, and how it is defended

This unit teaches a war that involved slavery, ethnic violence, and two massacres. The lines below
are drawn deliberately and are stated in the [admin memo](assessment/admin-preclearance-memo.md)
you should hand your principal **before** you teach Session 4.

- **No student embodies an enslaved person. No enslavement is a game mechanic.** Slavery is present
  as documented cause, as constitutional text students read in their own words (handout 11), and as
  narrative fact — including that some enslaved Texans fled *toward* Mexican lines during the
  Runaway Scrape, because for them the advancing enemy was the direction of freedom. It is never
  something anyone plays or optimizes. Every documented real-world classroom disaster in this area
  involved embodiment or transactional mechanics.
- **Never cast companies by student ethnicity, surname, or language background.** This is the most
  consistently flagged failure mode in the research. Cross-cast deliberately.
- **Slavery-as-cause is taught as a live historians' debate with names attached** — Barker and
  Campbell on one side, Lack and Torget on the other. Students evaluate the disagreement; the
  campaign does not settle it for them. That *is* TEKS 7.20(F).
- **Atrocity is even-handed.** Goliad is taught at full weight. So is the fact that Texian soldiers
  killed surrendering Mexican troops at San Jacinto while their own officers shouted at them to
  stop.
- **Tejanos are not a team.** Company 2 exists specifically to make an "Anglo vs. Mexican" reading
  impossible. They fight for Texas for six sessions and are driven out of it in Session 6.

## Assessment

A **two-strand rubric** ([`assessment/two-strand-rubric.md`](assessment/two-strand-rubric.md))
scores **Play** and **History** separately — so a quiet student who spends both Words well can earn
full marks, and a confident student with bad history cannot.

Graded artifacts are all in-fiction: the per-session **Dispatch** (2–3 sentences in character,
functioning as an exit ticket that doesn't feel like one) and **The Final Account**, an
out-of-character claim-evidence-reasoning piece answering *was the Texas Revolution inevitable?*

## Standards

Full verbatim alignment: [`research/teks-alignment.md`](research/teks-alignment.md).

**Core:** 7.2(D)(E)(F) · 7.3(A)(B)(C) · 7.4(A)(B)(C) · 7.13(A)(B) · 7.16 · 7.18(B) ·
**7.20(A)(B)(D)(E)(F)** — plus ELAR 7.1, 7.11(C), 7.12 and ELPS §120.21(d)(2).

**Two standards notes worth knowing:**
- **ELPS §74.4 is superseded.** The operative standard is **19 TAC §120.21 (Adopted 2024)**, with
  five proficiency levels rather than four. Many district documents still cite the old one.
- **A full Social Studies TEKS rewrite was adopted by the SBOE in June 2026**, reportedly moving
  Texas History to an 8th-grade capstone. Implementation is unconfirmed before 2027–2028 and the
  filed rule text could not be located. **Build against the 2022 TEKS; re-verify in spring 2027.**

**What this unit deliberately does NOT teach** — because roleplay is a poor vehicle for it, and
pretending otherwise weakens the alignment claim: the bare-date recall in 7.1(B) (handled by direct
instruction in Session 6), the abstract civics vocabulary in 7.13–7.14, the post-Revolution figures
in 7.17, and formal citation mechanics. §5 of the TEKS file lists each gap with a suggested
supplement.
